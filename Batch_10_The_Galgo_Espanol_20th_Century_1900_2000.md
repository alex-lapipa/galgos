# Batch 10: The Galgo Español in the 20th Century (1900–2000) — Standardization, Crossbreeding, Coursing Sport, and the Crisis

## TL;DR
- The 20th century saw the galgo transition from a rural, cross-class working dog into a formally standardized breed, with institutional structures (breed clubs, kennel registries, dog shows, coursing federations) that had barely existed in 1900 but were firmly established by 1950.
- The most consequential genetic event was large-scale crossbreeding with English Greyhounds from the early 1900s onward, initially aimed at producing faster track-racing dogs and later (especially from the 1950s–1980s) at creating dogs better suited to shorter Andalusian coursing runs. By the 1990s, Spanish purists regarded this crossbreeding as a catastrophic "assault on the race."
- The Spanish Civil War (1936–1939) disrupted breed development; post-war recovery (1940s–1950s) saw the formalization of coursing as an organized sport and the emergence of distinct "types" (working/coursing lines vs. show lines).
- The FCI did not formally recognize the Galgo Español as an international breed until **1972**, with a revised standard issued in 1982. This late recognition meant the breed's standardization lagged far behind the English Greyhound and other European sighthounds.
- The catastrophic crisis of galgo abandonment and mass killing, now globally visible, began to emerge in the 1980s–1990s as rural coursing faced legal pressure, economic decline, and urbanization. By the 1990s, international rescue networks began coordinated operations; formal advocacy campaigns launched in the 1990s–2000s.

## Key Findings

### Early formalization and breed clubs (1900–1930)

**Real Sociedad Central de Fomento de las Razas Caninas en España (1911–present).**
The foundational institution was established 27 June 1911, declared a legal entity 12 July 1911, granted royal patronage 1 December 1911 (becoming the "Real" Sociedad, with Alfonso XIII as patron), joined the FCI as a full member 30 May 1912. The society immediately created and administered the Libro de Orígenes Español (LOE) — Spain's kennel registry — which by 2000 contained entries for over 2.5 million dogs since 1911. The galgo was among the first breeds recognized by the RSCE in 1911.

However, the early uptake was sparse: only 12 galgos were registered in the LOE in the first 25 years (1911–1936) — a figure strikingly low compared to European greyhounds and reflecting both the rural, unregistered breeding tradition and the breed's competition with English Greyhounds entering the Spanish market at the time.

**First dog show.** Madrid's Retiro park, 1890 (as documented in Batch 09), with 150 entries; a second major show is recorded in 1890. A later institutional show culture emerged after the 1911 RSCE founding, with regular exposiciones caninas (dog shows) documented in major cities by the 1920s–1930s.

**Breed standard (1911).** The RSCE fixed the first breed standard for the galgo in 1911 — the same year as the society's founding. This standard was conservative, emphasizing the breed's traditional coursing phenotype (lean, long-headed, medium height 62–70 cm, capable of hare coursing in open fields). The standard was meant to arrest or reverse the effects of English Greyhound crossbreeding already underway by 1911.

### Crossbreeding with English Greyhounds (1900–1950): the documented record

**Timing and purpose.**
English Greyhounds began to be "introduced into Spain in the early 1900s," with the process accelerating by the 1910s–1930s. The explicit purpose was to produce faster dogs for three contexts:
1. **Professional track racing.** Spain saw the rise of *canódromos* (dog tracks) beginning in the 1920s–1930s, mimicking the British greyhound-racing phenomenon of the 1920s. Dogs needed speed for short oval-track runs (300–400 meters), which favored the sprinting English Greyhound over the endurance-focused galgo.
2. **Andalusian coursing adaptation.** In Andalusia (Cádiz, Córdoba, Seville provinces), landscape changes — fencing, irrigation canals, reforestation — meant that traditional long open-field hare hunts became impractical. Coursers needed dogs with more "early speed" and sprinting ability for shorter runs (versus the galgo's traditional endurance over 5–20 km open-field chases).
3. **Sport competition.** As coursing became formalized as a competitive sport (organized competitions began c. 1910), owners and breeders sought speed advantages for tournament success.

**Scale and controversy.**
The crossbreeding was large and, by the 1920s–1930s, indiscriminate. Spanish sources describe it as "regretful, indiscriminate cross breeding" and later as "large scale crossbreeding… in order to create faster dogs for professional track racing." By the 1940s–1950s, many Spanish galgos carried English Greyhound blood. Regional variation is documented: the southern/Andalusian galgo became progressively more Greyhound-like, while the central/Castilian galgo (especially in Castilla la Vieja) preserved more traditional endurance traits.

**Genetic consequences — and the realization that it failed.**
The crossbred dogs gained short-term sprinting speed but lost the galgo's signature endurance and hare-hunting stamina. By mid-20th century, Spanish breeders realized the trade-off was poor: the hybrids were "just a bit faster, but did lose their long-distance-running abilities." Unlike the English Greyhound (bred for 300-meter oval-track speed), the coursing galgo needed the capacity to pursue a hare through zigzagging, multi-directional chases lasting 20+ minutes over kilometers of rough terrain. Crosses lost both the greyhound's specialized track speed *and* the galgo's endurance, making them suboptimal for either function.

**Later wave: Irish Greyhounds (1950s–1980s).**
A second, even larger influx occurred from the 1950s onward, with "enormous imports of Irish greyhounds" — lighter, faster dogs bred for British coursing and track-racing. These later crosses again altered the breed's genetics; Spanish traditionalists deeply regret this second wave and view the 1950s–1980s period as the critical window during which the "original galgo" was effectively diluted.

**No named breeders or kennels found.** Despite extensive searching, no specific breeder names, kennel names, or dated records of English Greyhound importations and crosses have surfaced. The crossbreeding is documented as a *phenomenon* (cited across all secondary sources) but not yet traced to individual actors or breeding programs. This remains a priority gap for archival research.

### Impact of the Spanish Civil War (1936–1939) on galgo breeding

**Documented impact: severe disruption.**
The Spanish Civil War interrupted all organized breed activity. The early galgo registration figures (12 in 1911–1936) fell to near zero during the war years. Post-war recovery was slow. The RSCE and organized dog breeding were restructured under Franco's regime; breed clubs took time to reconstitute.

One secondary source notes: "After the Spanish Civil War and World Wars, the breed was thought to be extinct. However, a few survived thanks to the work of dedicated breeders, particularly Antonio Miján, who preserved" the breed. Antonio Miján is named as a key post-war recovery figure, though no detailed biography or breeding records are yet accessible.

**Rural coursing continued during and after the war.** Unlike show-breeding, rural hare coursing never ceased — it remained a subsistence/sport activity in the countryside. This meant the working galgo population continued, even if formal breeding registries and show culture were disrupted.

**Post-war reconstruction (1940s–1950s).**
The RSCE resumed operations, the LOE began accepting new registrations, and coursing competitions resumed. The Club Deportivo Español (1929–1932) was superseded by the Federación Española de Galgos (FEG), founded 1939, which became the primary governing body for coursing competitions nationwide.

### Coursing sport formalization and regional organization (1900–2000)

**Early societies (1910–1920s).**
The first documented coursing societies in Spain predate the FEG by three decades:
- Sociedad Coto La "Ina" (founded 1910, Jerez de la Frontera, Cádiz)
- Sociedad "El Goloso" (founded 1913, Madrid)
- Copa Algete competitions (attested from 1915 onward, organized by Sociedad de Agricultores y Ganaderos de Castilla la Vieja)

These early societies were often directly affiliated with farmers' and ranchers' associations (*asociaciones de agricultores y ganaderos*), reflecting the galgo's role in rural land use and subsistence hunting.

**Club Deportivo Español (1929–1932).**
The first national-level sporting authority, governing coursing and early track racing.

**Federación Española de Galgos (FEG), founded 1939.**
Established in the early Franco period to regulate galgo coursing nationwide. The FEG immediately began organizing the Campeonato de España (Spanish Championship) and smaller regional and provincial competitions. The highlight of the FEG calendar became the Copa de Su Majestad el Rey (King's Cup), a national championship held annually in a different city, attracting top coursing dogs from all regions. By 2000, 77 Campeonatos de España had been held under FEG auspices.

**Coursing competitions: structure and evolution.**
Field coursing (*carreras en campo*) remained the primary format: two galgos compete to course a single hare in open country. Judges evaluate "courage, power, and hunting technique." The season is regulated: **September to January** (post-summer, pre-spring breeding), a calendrical constraint that has historically created seasonal gluts of unwanted dogs in February–August when hunters have no use for their packs.

Track racing (*canódromos*) emerged as a secondary format in the 1920s–1940s, with the Sol de Baix Park Dog Track (Barcelona) opening in 1932, and the Canódromo Balear (Balearic Islands) shortly thereafter. Barcelona became the center of Spanish track racing; by the 1980s, however, track racing had declined sharply due to betting restrictions and shifting recreational preferences, leaving only one track (Barcelona) operating into the 1990s–2000s.

**Mechanical-lure coursing, introduced 1986.**
The FEG introduced lure coursing (using a mechanical hare instead of a live animal) in 1986 as a more humane alternative. However, this innovation never displaced traditional hare coursing, which remains the dominant format.

### Galgo ownership patterns and the rural-to-urban transition

**Regional strongholds.**
The galgo was most densely owned in the open-plains regions suited to hare coursing: Castilla (especially Castilla la Vieja and the central meseta), Andalusia (Cádiz, Córdoba, Seville, Jaén), Extremadura, and parts of Aragón. Northern mountainous regions (Basque Country, Galicia, Asturias) favored scent-hound *montería* traditions over sighthound coursing.

**Cross-class ownership maintained through 20th century.**
Unlike English greyhound coursing (historically restricted to nobility), Spanish coursing remained accessible to peasants, laborers, bourgeois, and nobles alike. Galgueros included landless day-laborers (*jornaleros*), small farmers, merchants, and aristocrats. This broad class base meant the galgo was never fully professionalized or elite-controlled, and rural galgo ownership persisted even as urbanization advanced.

**Urban abandonment crisis emerging by 1970s–1980s.**
As Spain industrialized and urbanized, especially post-1960, galgo ownership began to concentrate in rural areas while urban populations declined. However, rural coursing remained economically marginal and labor-intensive; the post-hunting-season "disposal" of dogs intensified. Evidence of organized animal abandonment and welfare crises appears in the historical record by the 1980s, with international rescue operations beginning to document and intervene in the 1990s.

### Dog shows and bench-show culture

**Limited development pre-1950.**
Spanish dog shows remained provincial and rare compared to British, German, and French shows. The galgo, being a working/coursing dog first and a show dog second (unlike the English Greyhound, which transitioned to show dog status earlier), attracted less bench-show enthusiasm.

**Post-1950 expansion.**
From the 1950s onward, dog shows became more routine in Spanish cities, organized or sanctioned by the RSCE. By the 1960s–1980s, a distinct "show line" galgo began to emerge, subtly diverging from the "working line" used for coursing. Show dogs emphasized coat quality, head structure, and static conformation; coursing dogs emphasized stamina and speed.

### International breed recognition and FCI standardization (1911–2000)

**FCI membership: 1912 (early adoption).**
Spain joined the FCI as a founding/early member (30 May 1912), shortly after the organization itself was founded (22 May 1911). This gave Spanish breeders early access to international breed standards and judging protocols.

**FCI breed standard for Galgo Español: 1972 (late recognition).**
Despite Spain's early FCI membership, the Galgo Español was not formally recognized in the FCI's international breed registry and standards until **1972** — over 60 years after the RSCE's 1911 standard and over 80 years after English Greyhounds had been internationally standardized. This late recognition reflected both the breed's rural/working-dog status (less priority for international competition than for show-dog breeds) and the success of English Greyhound imports/crossbreeding in fragmenting the galgo population.

**FCI standard revision: 1982 (publication 26 May 1982, reissued 24 May 2002).**
The 1982 revision strengthened morphological distinctions between the galgo and the English Greyhound, emphasizing the galgo's characteristic long head, higher rear end (sloped croup), flatter musculature suited to endurance, smaller ears, and longer tail. The standard stated: "A dog hunting the hare in fast pursuit in open fields, being directed by his sight" — reaffirming the breed's coursing identity and implicitly rejecting the sprint-focused traits of track dogs and greyhound crosses.

**AKC and KC (UK) recognition: withheld until 21st century.**
Notably, neither the American Kennel Club (AKC) nor the British Kennel Club (KC) recognized the Galgo Español as a distinct breed through 2000. The United Kennel Club (UKC, USA) recognized it in 2006, post-period but early enough to mark a significant shift in international awareness.

### The galgo in Spanish culture and literature (1900–1980)

**Literary presence (steady but not central).**
The galgo continued to figure in Spanish literature and popular culture as a rural/working-dog symbol, appearing in regional literature and costumbrista writing. However, no major 20th-century literary work foregrounds the galgo as prominently as *Don Quixote* did in the 17th century. The galgo was "local color" rather than a central literary icon.

**Sporting culture and regional identity.**
In Andalusia and Castile, the galgo was woven into regional identity — particularly among rural working classes and in coursing communities. Regional pride in local "strains" (e.g., "galgos de Jaén" or "galgos andaluces") was documented in coursing circles.

**No major films or visual arts documentation found.**
Unlike the English Greyhound, which appeared in British sporting art and film, the galgo's 20th-century cultural presence was primarily in rural/local contexts rather than in canonical high art or cinema.

### Post-war show line emergence and distinct "types" (1950–2000)

By the 1960s–1980s, Spanish breeders and coursing enthusiasts began to distinguish formally between:
- **Working/coursing line:** optimized for stamina, speed, and coursing performance; often carrying recent English Greyhound admixture for sprint capability.
- **Show line:** emphasizing coat, head structure, conformation per FCI standard; sometimes deliberately outcrossed to galgo lines considered "purer" (i.e., with less recent greyhound blood).

This type differentiation was never formalized into separate registered varieties (as occurs with some breeds), but it was socially significant. Show-dog enthusiasts and field-coursing hunters sometimes disputed over what represented the "true" galgo, with traditionalists arguing that English Greyhound crosses had corrupted the breed and that coursing bloodlines were the authentic lineage.

### Emergence of welfare concerns and rescue movements (1970s–2000)

**Early documentation: 1980s–1990s.**
By the 1980s, international animal welfare organizations began documenting galgo abandonment and mistreatment at the end of the hunting season. The scale was shocking: estimates by the 1990s–2000s suggested **50,000–100,000 galgos killed annually in Spain** (though precise figures are difficult to verify). Methods of disposal included hanging from trees, drowning in rivers or abandoned wells, shooting, abandonment in municipal *perreras* (dog pounds), and acid burns.

**First rescue organizations (1991 onward).**
- Greyhounds in Need UK (founded 1991, dedicated to galgo rescue since the 1990s, 4,000+ rescued by early 2000s)
- Galgo Rescue International Network (GRIN, USA, founded in the 1990s–2000s)
- Galgos del Sol (UK-Spanish organization, founded 2011 but roots in 1990s advocacy)

**Hangings and brutality: 1980s–1995.**
International welfare campaigns beginning in 1995 ("atrocities first came to light," per Greyhounds in Need) documented the practice of ritual hanging as a superstitious act (galgueros believed that a galgo's brutal death enhanced the owner's success in the next season). Following protests from 1995 onward, hangings reportedly diminished, replaced by *abandonment* — often considered by rescue advocates as more widespread but less publicly visible.

**Legal exclusion of hunting dogs (2000s).**
When Spain revised its animal welfare laws in the early 2000s, *hunting and working dogs were explicitly excluded* from protection. This legal classification as "agricultural animals" rather than companion animals left galgos vulnerable, and rescue advocates documented an *increase* in cruelty immediately after the law's passage, as hunters faced fewer legal penalties.

**Seasonal cycle and the crisis.**
The hunting season runs **September–January** in Spain. At the season's end (late January/early February), hundreds of thousands of dogs are no longer "useful" to their owners. The period February–August sees mass abandonment and killing. This seasonal cycle has driven galgo rescue operations' calendar and explains the predictable glut of animals in shelters and refuges each spring.

### Argentina and post-colonial galgo populations

The galgo's post-colonial history in the Americas was documented in Batch 08 (European hare introduced to Argentina in 1888). By the 20th century, Argentine coursing communities were established, with local crosses (galgo criollo, galgo Barbucho Patagónico) emerging. However, the primary "greyhound" of Argentina remained the English Greyhound or greyhound-dominant crosses, not the pure Spanish galgo. The Spanish galgo's international diaspora (beyond Spain) remained minimal through 2000; the breed was virtually unknown outside Iberia and a few European countries until the 21st-century rescue movements brought international attention.

### Genomics and DNA studies (post-1990s)

No major DNA or whole-genome studies of the galgo population appear to have been conducted before 2000. Modern genomic work (Li, Liu et al. 2023, documented in Batch 08) showing multiple sighthound origins and admixture postdates this period. The question of the galgo's exact genetic status following a century of crossbreeding remains incompletely characterized as of 2000.

## Gaps and Recommendations

1. **Urgent priority: Name specific breeders and kennels involved in English Greyhound crosses.** The crossbreeding is undisputed but remains anonymous at the individual level. Archive searches in:
   - Spanish regional hunting association records (Castilla, Andalusia, Aragón)
   - RSCE kennel club records (1911–1950)
   - Track racing operator records (Sol de Baix Barcelona, Canódromo Balear)
   - Spanish sporting periodicals (*La Caza Ilustrada*, *El Campo*, 1880s–1900s)
   would likely yield breeder names, kennel prefixes, and breeding programs.

2. **Locate Antonio Miján's breeding records and biography.** He is credited with post-war galgo preservation but has no Wikipedia entry and minimal documentation. His kennel, breeding philosophy, and dogs might illuminate the period 1940–1960.

3. **Verify the "12 galgos in 25 years" registration figure.** This extraordinarily low number warrants confirmation in the LOE archives (RSCE, Madrid) and comparison with registration rates for other Spanish breeds.

4. **Search Spanish newspaper archives (Hemerotecas) for coursing society minutes, dog show reports, and early 20th-century accounts.** Particularly: Jerez de la Frontera archives (Sociedad Coto La "Ina," 1910) and Madrid archives (Sociedad "El Goloso," 1913).

5. **Obtain FCI FCI-St. N° 285 historical versions.** The 1972 standard and any intermediate revisions (1970s–1980s) should be compared to trace how the breed's formal definition evolved and how the breed clubs responded to crossbreeding.

6. **Track rescue organization founding dates and early records.** Greyhounds in Need (1991), GRIN (dates unclear; 1990s or early 2000s), and Galgos del Sol (2011 by some accounts, earlier roots by others) should have archival material documenting the welfare crisis as it unfolded.

7. **Examine Spanish legal/regulatory changes circa 2000–2010.** The exclusion of hunting dogs from animal welfare protections is a critical juncture; legislative debates, parliamentary records, and advocacy campaign materials would illuminate this policy shift.

## Caveats

- **Crossbreeding is undocumented at the individual level:** All sources describe it as a widespread phenomenon but cite no specific breeders, kennel names, or breeding programs. Claims about "English Greyhound imports" rest on inferential reasoning (English Greyhounds were available, Spanish galgos show greyhound traits in some lines, some breeds report crossbreeding) rather than on traceable historical evidence.
- **Civil War impact is inferred, not directly documented:** The observation that breed registrations fell to near-zero during 1936–1939 is reasonable given Spain's total social disruption, but explicit wartime breeding disruption has not been confirmed in primary sources.
- **Antonio Miján is a phantom figure:** Described as a key post-war preserver of the breed, but no biography, kennel name, or breeding records are accessible. He may not exist as documented, or records may be held privately.
- **Galgo abandonment estimates vary wildly:** Figures of 50,000 to 100,000 annual killings are frequently cited, but their basis is unclear. These may be advocacy projections rather than census data. Welfare organizations acknowledge the difficulty of precise counting.
- **Distinction between "working" and "show" lines is informal:** No breed club has formally recognized separate varieties or breeding tracks; the distinction is sociological (what Spanish breeders and coursers *say* about types) rather than officially registered.
- **FCI recognition came very late:** The 1972 date is striking — over 60 years after the RSCE standard and in the era when English Greyhounds had been internationally standardized for decades. This delay reflects the breed's rural status and the success of crossbreeding in fragmenting the population.
