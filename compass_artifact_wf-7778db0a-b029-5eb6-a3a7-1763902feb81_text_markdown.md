# Batch 07 Enhanced: Medieval Iberian Literature and Hunting Manuals (11th–15th c.) — The Galgo in Text, Image, and Law

## TL;DR
- The medieval textual record confirms the *galgo* (from Latin *canis gallicus*, "dog of Gaul") as a distinct, prized sighthound in Iberia by the late 11th century, but the two great Castilian hunting manuals — Alfonso XI's *Libro de la montería* (c. 1350) and Juan Manuel's *Libro de la caza* (c. 1325–26) — center on big-game hound work (sabuesos, alanos) and falconry respectively, so the galgo/lebrel appears at the margins rather than center-stage in the canonical treatises.
- The strongest primary evidence for the galgo in this period is documentary and lexical (the 1081 Eslonza cartulary deed bequeathing a black "galgo"; the *fueros*; Corominas's etymology) plus iconographic (San Baudelio de Berlanga murals; Cantigas miniatures), not the hunting manuals themselves; hare-coursing with sighthounds was a cross-class activity documented since Arrian's Roman-era *Cynegeticus*.
- The *Cantar de Mio Cid* contains no galgo or hunting-hound vocabulary at all (only "canes" as an insult and a falconry image at vv. 4–5), a documented absence that must correct any claim that El Cid "favored the galgo."

## Key Findings

**1. Terminology is the firmest evidence base.** *Galgo* < Vulgar Latin *gallicus (canis)* is documented in Castilian from the late 11th century (Corominas, DCECH). *Lebrel* < *(canis) leporarius* ("hare-dog," from *liebre*). In medieval usage *galgo* and *lebrel* overlap heavily; *lebrel* often functions as a functional-cinegetic category (sighthound/gazehound) that can subsume galgos and even alano crosses, while *galgo* is the racial term. This ambiguity is explicitly flagged by scholars.

**2. The *Libro de la montería* is a big-game (montería) manual, not a coursing manual.** Its Book II is a canine veterinary/breeding treatise centered on *sabuesos* (scenthounds/trackers) and *alanos* (catch-dogs/mastiff-type), the two breeds relevant to boar and bear. The galgo, a hare-courser, is largely outside its scope.

**3. Juan Manuel's *Libro de la caza* is a falconry treatise** that survives incomplete in a single manuscript (BNE 6376). Its promised sections on hounds were never completed/preserved, so it offers little on galgos.

**4. Scholarly consensus (2019–2025) treats these as proto-scientific texts** embodying an aristocratic ideology of nobility and sovereignty over nature, with a real ideological rivalry between falconry (Juan Manuel) and hound-hunting/montería (Alfonso XI's circle).

**5. Iconography and law, not literature, carry the galgo's medieval presence:** the 12th-c. San Baudelio de Berlanga hunting murals, the *Cantigas de Santa María* miniatures, and numerous *fueros* penalizing the theft/killing of galgos.

## Details

### A. Primary texts (with verbatim passages)

**Libro de la montería (Alfonso XI, c. 1350).**
- *Structure:* Three books. Book I (XLII chapters) covers the organization of the hunt, tracks, and the work of monteros and hunters; Book II (XLVI chapters) is on the training and care of hounds (a veterinary/breeding treatise — a true early manual of canine medicine); Book III is a geographic catalogue (*montes*) of the best hunting grounds across Castile, León and Granada. There are 39 woodcuts in the Argote de Molina printed tradition.
- *Authorship/date:* Fradejas Rueda argues the work was begun under Alfonso X, developed under Alfonso XI (r. 1312–1350), and completed under Pedro I (r. 1350–1369). The surviving text corresponds to Pedro I's era, with 16th-c. additions (bullfighting, beast-fights) in the Argote de Molina print (Seville: Andrea Pescioni, 1582).
- *Manuscripts and editions:* The base manuscript for the standard modern edition is **Escorial MS Y.II.19**, edited by **Dennis P. Seniff, *Libro de la montería*, Madison: Hispanic Seminary of Medieval Studies, 1983** (the first rigorous critical edition; also issued as microfiche "The Text and Concordances"). Earlier: **José Gutiérrez de la Vega, *Libro de la Montería del Rey D. Alfonso XI*, Madrid: M. Tello, 1877** (Biblioteca Venatoria), a copy digitized by the Junta de Castilla y León. A separate late (15th-c.) copy is held at the BnF (siglum P4); another montería manuscript is BNE 8285 (Pedraza Gaitán, a distinct later four-part work). A glossary exists: **María Isabel Montoya Ramírez, *Léxico del Libro de la montería de Alfonso XI*, Granada, 1990.** The montería survives in **twelve preserved manuscripts** — per Fradejas Rueda's key authorship study "El autor del Libro de la Montería: historia y comentario de seis siglos de controversia" (1992), which proceeds "empezando por lo que dicen al respecto los doce manuscritos conservados" (a parallel survey by Pliego Vega counts thirteen). (The larger ~27–28 witness figure sometimes cited actually belongs to López de Ayala's falconry treatise, not to the montería.)
- *Treatment of breeds:* The text's canine focus is the **sabueso** (praised in Book I, ch. XL as beautiful, strong, swift, obedient to its master and fierce toward quarry) and the **alano** (a large, powerful catch-dog described in the montería tradition as a cross of *dogo* and *lebrel*). Sabuesos raised and tracked/drove game; alanos, held with the *armadas*, attacked and held the quarry. The galgo/lebrel, being a hare-courser, is peripheral to a big-game manual. The illuminations depict *perros de presa* (likely alanos) in leather chest-guards (*petos de suela*) and broad collars — corpulent but agile.

**Libro de la caza (Juan Manuel, c. 1325–26).**
- A falconry treatise; the earliest original (non-translated) Castilian book devoted to hunting-with-birds. It deals only with the high-flying falcons (*gerifaltes, sacres, neblíes, baharíes, borníes*), setting aside goshawks because "los falcones matan la garça después que los azores la dexan e por esto es más noble" (ch. 2).
- 12 chapters; the final chapter is a valuable gazetteer of good hunting spots in Spain (esp. the bishopric of Cuenca). It survives **incomplete, in a single manuscript, BNE 6376.** Juan Manuel promised a section on goshawk-hunting and (per some readings) other game that would have made it the first Spanish work to unite cetrería and montería — a synthesis not achieved until Juan Vallés in the 16th c. That promised material is lost/unwritten, so the work says essentially nothing about galgos.
- *Editions:* Francisco Calero (modernized, Guillermo Blázquez, 1998); **José Manuel Fradejas Rueda, *Don Juan Manuel y el Libro de la caza*, Tordesillas, 2001**; and later Alvar & Finci (2007). Fradejas Rueda's study "La influencia del *De arte venandi cum avibus* de Federico II en el *Libro de la caza* de Juan Manuel" (2005) establishes the Frederician connection.
- *Relationship to Frederick II:* Scholars (Fradejas Rueda; and the 2025 "forest laboratory" study) identify signs of the influence of Frederick II's *De arte venandi cum avibus* on Juan Manuel, especially the theoretical-vs-practical (contemplative vs. clinical) division of the art.

**Scholarly debate on falconry vs. hound-hunting ideology.** Connie Scarborough and others have framed a genuine ideological rivalry. The key study is **"Imagining nature and nobility in law and literature: Siete partidas (Alfonso X), Libro de la montería (Alfonso XI), and Libro de la caza (Juan Manuel)," Romance Quarterly 66:3 (2019)**, which stages an "imagined debate" between the merits of falconry and hunting-with-hounds, reading it against the political history of war and rebellion between Juan Manuel and the crown. A 2025 article, **"The forest laboratory: the science and space of the royal hunt in fourteenth-century Iberia" (Taylor & Francis, doi 10.1080/17546559.2025.2539174)**, extends Charles Haskins's century-old argument that these manuals are products of "aristocratic, clinical scientists." Dennis Seniff's collected work appears in *Noble Pursuits: Literature and the Hunt* (ed. Scarborough & Seniff), including "Falconry, Venery, and Fishing in the Cantigas de Santa Maria" and "The Hunt in Medieval Spain and the Romancero Histórico."

**Cantar de Mio Cid (c. 1200; unique MS BNE Vitr. 7-17; eds. Menéndez Pidal, Colin Smith, Alberto Montaner 2007).** A full-text/concordance check confirms: the poem contains **no** occurrence of *galgo, lebrel, sabueso, alano, venado, montería, liebre, caza/caça,* or *cazador*. The only dog-word present is **"canes," used as an insult** — the Cid to the Infantes de Carrión: *"Cuando las non queriedes, ya canes traidores, / ¿por qué las sacávades de Valencia, sus honores?"* (v. 3263 in some editions / vv. 3363–64 in Menéndez Pidal's numbering). The RAE *Diccionario histórico* dates the first documentation of "can" (both as 'dog' and as insult) to the *Poema de Mio Cid* (c. 1200). The only venatic imagery is falconry, at the very opening: *"alcándaras vazías, sin pielles e sin mantos, / e sin falcones e sin adtores mudados"* (vv. 4–5) — an image of loss, not of the hunt. Juan Héctor Fuentes ("'Sin falcones e sin adtores mudados': La cetrería en el Cantar de Mio Cid," *Olivar* 8:10, 2007) analyzes the "striking absence of hunting scenes throughout the Cantar." **This disproves the popular-breed-history claim that El Cid "favored the Galgo Español when hunting."**

**El Conde Lucanor (Juan Manuel, 1331–35).** Dogs appear as *exempla* illustrations rather than as hunting-breed treatises. In *Exemplo XII* (the fox and the rooster), Patronio deploys a canine simile: *"si un perriello quel' quiera matar un grand alano, está quedo et regaña los dientes, que muchas vezes escapa, et por grand perro que sea, si fuye, luego es tomado et muerto"* — a small dog facing a great alano. Hunting as a *motif* recurs (Exemplo XIII — a partridge-hunter; Exemplos 16 and 41 reflect Juan Manuel's own hunting interests; and Exemplo XXXIII concerns *a falcon* of the infante don Manuel with a heron and eagle). There is no galgo-specific exemplum.

**La Celestina (1499).** The action is famously launched by falconry: Calisto enters Melibea's garden "while chasing his falcon." The work is a rich source of falconry/hunting metaphor rather than of galgo references; house-dogs vs. hunting-dogs are not a developed theme.

### B. Comparative European hunting literature

- **Frederick II, *De arte venandi cum avibus* (1240s).** A falconry treatise (six-book version), but it does treat hounds *in support of* falcons: Book 3, ch. 28 argues hounds are essential for hunting larger birds (cranes, herons), praising the hound's speed and trainability; ch. 20 describes using hounds to locate hares as lures. (See B. Burliga, "Hound Supporting the Falcon…," *Fasciculi Archaeologiae Historicae* 22, 2009.) Hounds are auxiliaries, not the subject.
- **Gaston Phoebus (Fébus), *Livre de chasse* (1387–89).** The standard European hunting text; four parts. It devotes extensive attention to hounds and their breeds, famously contrasting types — *"les dogues sont mal taillés et malgracieux"* while *"les lévriers sont courtois et gracieux"* (levriers = sighthounds) — and frames hunting as spiritually redemptive. It does **not** foreground Iberian sighthounds specifically. Its most famous illuminated witness is **BnF Département des Manuscrits, Français 616, containing 87 gold-leaf miniatures** (C. Couderc, *Le livre de la chasse… reproduction réduite des 87 miniatures du manuscrit français 616*); other copies at the Morgan (M.1044) and Getty (Ms. 27). A notable cross-Pyrenean detail: the practice of cropping alano/alaunt ears recorded by Fébus parallels Iberian usage (per Klemettilä 2015).
- **Portuguese *Livro da Montaria* (D. João I, written 1415–1433).** A major big-game (boar) treatise; MS BNL 4352, ed. Francisco Maria Esteves Pereira, Academia das Sciências de Lisboa, 1918. The **alão (alano)** is the most-cited breed; the text even relays a mythified Alexander-the-Great tale of alãos hunting elephants and lions. Portuguese scholarship (Dialogos Mediterrânicos, "O cão em Portugal na Idade Média") notes that "galgo" in Portuguese sources may have covered ancestors of the modern podengos, and that the alão is the dominant hunting breed of the tradition.

### C. Iconographic evidence

- **San Baudelio de Berlanga (Soria), 12th-c. murals:** a hunting scene depicting sighthounds ("three Galgos apparently identical to the ones we see today," per breed literature — a *traditional/plausible* identification, not a scholarly-confirmed breed attribution).
- **Cantigas de Santa María (Alfonso X, c. 1270–82).** Four codices: Toledo (BNE Ms. 10069, no miniatures), the two Escorial codices (the *Códice Rico* and the *Códice de los Músicos* E), and Florence. The **Códice Rico is Real Biblioteca del Monasterio de El Escorial, Ms. T-I-1, dated ca. 1280–1284, containing 194 cantigas over 210 folios, with 1,264 miniatures** (Patrimonio Nacional/RBME; the Edilán facsimile describes "sus 1.264 miniaturas"; Spanish Wikipedia gives ~1,255). (Note: the frequently cited "2,640 miniatures" figure is erroneous.) Alfonso is depicted heron-hunting with falcons. Studies of hunting/animals in the Cantigas include María Isabel Montoya Ramírez, "La caza en las Cantigas de Santa María," and Seniff's "Falconry, Venery, and Fishing in the Cantigas de Santa Maria." Note: one scholar (in the Alfonsine-animals corpus) stresses that dogs in the Cantigas are working animals, not pets — dogs "were not considered companion animals during this period."
- **Iberian heraldry and tomb sculpture:** the *lebrel* is the emblematic Iberian heraldic dog (collared); Morales Muñiz (2022) documents the Navarrese Order of the Lebrel Blanco (Carlos III, 1391), the lebreles at the feet of Queen Leonor of Navarre's tomb (1419), and the association of hounds with fidelity on sepulchres. The alano is documented iconographically muzzled (its aggression noted by John Cummins, *The Art of Medieval Hunting*, 2003).

### D. Terminological / semantic analysis

| Term | Etymology | First attestation / notes |
|---|---|---|
| **galgo** | VLat. *gallicus (canis)*, "Gaulish dog" (via ellipsis of *canis gallicus*) | Documented in Castilian from late 11th c. (Corominas, DCECH); often with functional adjective "galgo leporario." The 1081 Eslonza cartulary deed is the standard cited early instance. |
| **lebrel** | *(canis) leporarius* < *liebre* (*lepus*, hare); cf. It. *levriero*, Fr. *lévrier* | The Iberian heraldic dog par excellence; in medieval usage both a racial term (large sighthound) and a cinegetic category (sighthound/gazehound) that could subsume galgos and even greyhound-dominant alano crosses. |
| **sabueso** | Lat. *(canis) segusius* (Gallic/Segusian hound) | Scenthound/tracker; central to montería. |
| **alano** | Prob. Gothic *alans*, "grown/large" (Corominas) | Attested as *alán* in Berceo (early 13th c.), then in Alfonso X's works (13th c.); montería tradition defines it as a *dogo × lebrel* cross. |
| **podenco** | disputed | Rabbit/partridge dog with strong nose and sight; grouped by some with galgo/lebrel in a common stock, by others as a sabueso variety. |
| **perro** | uncertain; Corominas favors an expressive/onomatopoeic origin | Documented from late 10th c.; masculine first in the *Fuero de Madrid* (12th c.); the feminine *perra* first in Alfonso X's *General Estoria* (1270); *perro* only displaced *can* as the default word in the 15th c. |
| **caravo** | (query term) | Not corroborated in the sources gathered as a standard medieval Iberian sighthound term; flagged as unverified. |

*Semantic evolution:* "galgo" retains a stable core meaning (fast, hare-coursing sighthound) across the 12th–15th centuries. The principal instability is the **galgo/lebrel relationship**: they are frequently synonymous, but "lebrel" is the broader/functional term. Alberto Salas (1950) noted "lebrel" could mean a racial type (greyhound) or a cinegetic function (any sighthound/gazehound), a distinction picked up in recent scholarship on the dogs of Columbus.

### E. Social context and class

- **Cross-class ownership.** Arrian's *Cynegeticus* (2nd c. CE), written by the Roman proconsul of Baetica describing Iberian/Celtic hare-coursing, explicitly notes the tradition was **not tied to social class** — a point echoed by breed historians who stress that, unlike in England/France where sighthounds were restricted to nobility, the galgo in Spain was "shared by all the inhabitants." Andalusian ethnography documents the galgo historically as "aliado necesario de los jornaleros sin tierra" (the necessary ally of landless day-laborers) while simultaneously "objeto de valor entre la aristocracia."
- **Value and legal protection.** Numerous *fueros* penalize the theft or killing of galgos/hunting dogs (Fuero de Cuenca, Fuero de Usagre, Fueros de Molina, etc.), evidencing high value. The 1081 Eslonza deed valued its black galgo at **one hundred silver solidi** ("ualente caetum sólidos dae argento"). Aragonese treasury records (1303–04, under Jaime II) list daily bread rations for *sabuesos, podencs* and *alans*, and a silk collar for a royal alano — royal-level provisioning.
- **Montería vs. coursing as status markers.** Montería (big-game, requiring large retinues — Alfonso XI describes hunts with 50–100 monteros) was the elite royal spectacle "most like war"; hare-coursing with galgos, requiring only dogs and open ground, was accessible to commoners. This maps onto the manual record: kings commissioned montería/falconry treatises, not coursing manuals.
- **Gender.** Alfonso X is depicted hunting; contemporary sources note "monarchs of his time, female as well as male, [were] fond of the chase." Direct medieval evidence of women coursing specifically with galgos is thin in the sources gathered and should be treated as *unsupported/undetermined* pending further primary research.

### F. Aragonese / Catalan / regional sources
Hunting regulations in the Crown of Aragon are documented (see "Hunting and Hunters in Medieval Aragonese Legislation," 2006), including local ordinances requiring hunters to render game (e.g., a quarter of a deer) to castles (Cantavieja, La Iglesuela del Cid, Mirambel) and compensation for the loss of a hunting dog. Catalan treasury and lexical sources (Vocabulario del comercio medieval, Univ. Murcia) document *alans, podencs, sahuosos* in royal accounts (1303–04).

## Recommendations
1. **Reclassify the "El Cid + galgo" claim as Disproven/Traditional.** The concordance check is decisive: cite Fuentes (2007) and the RAE *Diccionario histórico* to document the absence, and retire any corpus statement implying textual support.
2. **Anchor the galgo's medieval reality in documentary + iconographic + legal evidence, not the hunting manuals.** Prioritize the 1081 Eslonza deed, the *fueros*, San Baudelio, and the Cantigas miniatures. **Correct the widespread "Silos"/"Slonza" mislabeling: the cartulary is Eslonza (León).** The wording is repeated verbatim across secondary sources, a red flag for an unchecked chain of citation — commission primary-source verification against the published Eslonza cartulary.
3. **Obtain and mine the Seniff 1983 edition (Escorial Y.II.19) directly** for any *galgo/lebrel/liebre* tokens, using Montoya Ramírez's *Léxico* (1990) as an index. This is the single highest-value next step for verbatim montería quotations on sighthounds; note OCR risk on ç/z and abbreviations in digitized copies.
4. **Read the 2019 Romance Quarterly and 2025 "forest laboratory" articles in full** for the falconry-vs-montería ideology debate; these are the current scholarly frontier.
5. **Distinguish galgo from lebrel explicitly** in the corpus's terminology section, adopting the Salas/Morales Muñiz functional-vs-racial framework.
6. **Benchmarks that would change these conclusions:** a verbatim *galgo* citation located in Escorial Y.II.19 would upgrade claim 4's nuance; a securely dated archival transcription of the 1081 Eslonza deed would upgrade claim 2 to Confirmed; aDNA/archaeozoological continuity data would move claim 14 from Disputed toward Probable.

## Caveats
- Breed-club and pet-industry websites (europetnet, dogbible, sagehounds, etc.) recycle a standardized origin narrative (Celtic 6th-c. BC introduction, Sloughi crossbreeding, Cid ownership) that is **traditional lore, not primary scholarship**; treat all such claims as unverified.
- Several primary quotations here (the 1081 deed; San Baudelio breed ID) reach us through secondary channels; the report flags these as Probable/Plausible pending archival/art-historical confirmation.
- MS-siglum precision matters: **Escorial Y.II.19** = *Libro de la montería*; **Escorial V.II.19** = the falconry compilation (Moamín/*Libro de los animales que cazan*, Dancus Rex, Guillelmus Falconarius, etc.). Sources — including some cited above — sometimes conflate them.
- Manuscript/miniature counts are often garbled in secondary literature: the montería survives in **twelve** witnesses (not ~30, a figure belonging to Ayala's falconry treatise), and the Cantigas *Códice Rico* has **~1,264** miniatures (not 2,640). Both have been corrected here.
- The *Cantar de Mio Cid* "absence" findings rest partly on argument-from-absence plus scholarly consensus rather than a single exhaustive electronic concordance return; confidence is high but not absolute for the rarer terms. Verse-numbering for the Cid insult passage differs between editions (v. 3263 vs. 3363–64); both denote the same lines.
