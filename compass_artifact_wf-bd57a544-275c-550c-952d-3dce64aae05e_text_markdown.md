# Batch 03: Greece and Rome — Classical Textual and Archaeological Sources for the Galgo Español Project

## TL;DR
- The Greek and Roman sources establish a clear, citable literary trail for a Celtic sighthound (the *vertragus*) that hunted hares by sight and was coursed on horseback — Arrian's *Cynegeticus* (c. 136–146 CE) is by far the richest source — but **none of these authors describes a distinctively "Spanish" hound, and no ancient text asserts a lineage to the modern Galgo Español**; that continuity is a modern breed-literature inference, not an ancient claim.
- The much-repeated claim that Arrian "governed Baetica" and "hunted hares in Spain" is **substantially supported, not debunked**: modern prosopography (Syme, Bosworth, Stadter) accepts, on the strength of a Greek verse inscription to Artemis found at Córdoba, that Arrian was proconsul of Baetica c. 123–126 CE — but the inscription says nothing about hunting hares there, and the "he first saw the vertragus in Spain" story is a speculative embellishment.
- "Codex Romanicus" is **not a real Roman text on hunting dogs or territorial administration**; it appears to be a garbled popular reference, most plausibly conflating the *Cynegetica*/*Cynegeticus* tradition (or Pliny's encyclopedia) with the names of the late Roman legal codices (Gregorianus, Theodosianus, Justinianus).

## Key Findings

1. **Xenophon** (*Cynegeticus*, late 5th/early 4th c. BCE) describes scent-hunting with Laconian (Spartan) hounds and nets; he knows nothing of coursing by sight, a point Arrian makes explicitly.
2. **Grattius** (*Cynegeticon*, Augustan) gives the earliest Latin dog-breed catalogue by geographic origin (lines 150ff), naming the Celtic hounds and a swift yellow-spotted *Vertraha/Vertragus* "swifter than thought" (lines 202–208).
3. **Martial** (*Epigrams* XIV.200, the *Apophoreta*) gives the first explicit naming of the *vertragus*: "*Non sibi sed domino venatur vertragus acer / inlaesum leporem qui tibi dente feret*."
4. **Arrian** (*Cynegeticus*) is the single most important source: the *vertragus* is a Celtic sighthound named for its speed; he describes its appearance, temperament, Celtic hare-coursing on horseback, and sportsmanship (letting the hare go). His own hound was named *Hormé*. **He locates coursing among the Celts/Gauls, not in Hispania.**
5. **Pliny the Elder** (*Naturalis Historia* VIII.61–63, 147–150) treats the dog's loyalty, war-dogs, cross-breeding with wolves (Gauls) and tigers (India), and hunting sagacity; Book VIII.81 records Iberian rabbits, but he gives no "Spanish hound."
6. **Varro** (*De Re Rustica* II.9) discusses only guard/shepherd dogs, not hunting dogs.
7. **Columella** (*De Re Rustica* VII.12) systematically treats farm and shepherd dogs but deliberately omits the hunting dog as a distraction from farm work.
8. **Oppian/Pseudo-Oppian** (*Cynegetica*, Greek, dedicated to Caracalla, after 212 CE) gives an extensive breed catalogue (I.368–538), including an Iberian dam paired with a Sarmatian sire, and praises the Laconian for coursing gazelle, deer, and hare.
9. **Nemesianus** (*Cynegetica*, 283/284 CE) names Spartan, Molossian, British, Pannonian, and Iberian/Spanish-blooded hounds (lines 224ff) — an explicit mention of dogs of Spanish stock.
10. **Roman Iberian archaeology** does show coursing/sighthound scenes (hounds chasing hares, "galgos"/"lebreles") in 3rd–4th c. CE mosaics and frescoes at Conímbriga, Mérida, and Córdoba, but the big-game hunt (boar, felines) dominates and no scholar identifies the depicted dogs as the modern breed.
11. **"Codex Romanicus"** does not exist as a Roman hunting/administrative text.

## Details

### 1. Xenophon, *Cynegeticus* (Κυνηγετικός, "On Hunting")
Xenophon of Athens (c. 430–354 BCE), better known for the *Anabasis* and *Hellenica*, wrote the earliest surviving hunting treatise. Standard citation is by chapter and section (e.g., *Cyn.* III.1, IV.1). Greek text and E. C. Marchant's Loeb translation are digitized on Perseus (urn:cts:greekLit:tlg0032.tlg014; perseus.tufts.edu) and at ToposText (topostext.org/work/796).

- **Laconian hounds:** Xenophon describes two varieties of Laconian (Spartan) hound — the **Castorian** (named for Castor) and the **Vulpine** (*alopekis*, thought to be dog–fox crosses) (*Cyn.* III.1). The ideal Laconian is large, with a small head, straight nose, upright ears, long supple neck, black sparkling eyes; tan-and-white or black-and-tan (*Cyn.* IV.1, 7–8).
- **Scent-hunting, not sight-coursing:** These are scent hounds that "give chase vigorously without relaxing, with much clamor and baying... giving tongue properly again and again" (*Cyn.* IV.5). The prey is driven into nets strung across its path (*Cyn.* VI, X). For boar he prescribes Indian, Cretan, Locrian, and Laconian hounds plus nets, javelins, and spears (*Cyn.* X.1).
- **Dog names:** Xenophon recommends short (two-syllable) names and lists **exactly forty-seven** (*Cyn.* VII.5), per Marchant's Loeb text on Perseus: *Psyche, Thymus, Porpax, Styrax, Lonché, Lochus, Phrura, Phylax, Taxis, Xiphon, Phonax, Phlegon, Alcé, Teuchon, Hyleus, Medas, Porthon, Sperchon, Orgé, Bremon, Hybris, Thallon, Rhomé, Antheus, Hebe, Getheus, Chara, Leusson, Augo, Polys, Bia, Stichon, Spudé, Bryas, Oenas, Sterrus, Craugé, Caenon, Tyrbas, Sthenon, Aether, Actis, Aechmé, Noës, Gnomé, Stibon, Hormé.* The last of these, **Hormé** (Ὁρμή, "Impulse/Rush"), is the very name Arrian later gave his own vertragus.
- **The key point for this project:** Xenophon predates true sighthound coursing. His hounds catch the hare "contrary to the nature of the animal, by accident" (quoted and criticized by Arrian) — a remark Arrian seizes on to prove Xenophon did not know the fast Celtic dog.

### 2. Grattius, *Cynegeticon* (Augustan; poet fl. under Augustus, 63 BCE–14 CE)
Grattius (Grattius Faliscus) survives in **541 hexameters preserved in a single manuscript of c. 800 CE**, mutilated toward the end (Loeb: "one book of about 540 hexameters mutilated towards its end"). The only ancient reference to him is Ovid, *Ex Ponto* IV.16.34. Standard citation is by line number; the Loeb edition (J. Wight Duff & Arnold M. Duff, *Minor Latin Poets*, 1934) and LacusCurtius (penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Grattius) carry the full text and Duff translation.

- **Breed catalogue by origin (lines 150–170ff):** "Dogs belong to a thousand lands and they each have characteristics derived from their origin" (*mille canum patriae ductique ab origine mores / quisque suo*, lines 156–157). His list places Asiatic breeds before European, with the **Celtae ("Celtic dogs")** sandwiched between Medi and Geloni (lines 155–157). "The Median dog... is a great fighter, and great glory exalts the far-distant Celtic dogs" (line 156).
- **The vertragus (lines 202–208):** For light hunting of antelope or hare he recommends Petronian dogs, "swift Sycambrians and the **Vertraha** coloured with yellow spots — swifter than thought or a winged bird it runs, pressing hard on the beasts it has found, though less likely to find them when they lie hidden." The Loeb notes the MSS of Martial give *vertragus/vertagus/vetragus*, and connects the word to Arrian's Celtic etymology and Italian *veltro* ("greyhound").
- **The metagon (lines 213ff):** a tracking dog "mentioned only by Grattius," associated with Sparta and Crete.
- **Molossians (lines 179ff):** for serious combat "you could not admire the renowned Molossians so much."
- **Iberian connections:** Grattius mentions the flax of Spanish **Saetabis** for nets, and Spanish horses — the **Callaeci** (a people of Hispania Tarraconensis) whose horses "can traverse the jagged Pyrenees," though he would not trust a Spanish steed in the hunt (lines 514ff). These are geographic, not canine, references.

### 3. Martial, *Epigrams* XIV.200 (the *Apophoreta*)
Martial (M. Valerius Martialis, c. 38/41–104 CE), himself a Spaniard from Bilbilis, published Book XIV (the *Apophoreta*, "gifts to carry away," couplets for Saturnalia presents) c. 84–85 CE. The vertragus epigram is numbered **XIV.200** (=CC), titled *Canis vertragus*:

> **Latin (Heraeus/Loeb):** *Non sibi sed domino venatur vertragus acer, / inlaesum leporem qui tibi dente feret.*
> **English (Ker, Loeb):** "The keen vertragus hunts not for himself but for his master, and will bring you the hare unhurt in his teeth."

This is the **first explicit naming of the vertragus in Latin literature** and the source of the famous "hunts not for himself but for his master" tag. Neighboring epigrams describe an Asturian horse (XIV.199, *Asturco*) and a *catella Gallicana* (XIV.198), placing the vertragus in a run of luxury-import animals. Latin text at Perseus (Heraeus/Borovskij) and poesialatina.it; the standard modern commentary is T. J. Leary, *Martial Book XIV: The Apophoreta* (1996).

### 4. Arrian, *Cynegeticus* (Κυνηγετικός) — the central source
Lucius Flavius Arrianus of Nicomedia (c. 86–after 160 CE), the "Second Xenophon," wrote his *Cynegeticus* explicitly as a supplement to Xenophon's, styling himself after his namesake. The standard critical text is A. G. Roos, *Flavii Arriani quae exstant omnia* II (Teubner, 1928/1968), 76–102; standard citation is by chapter/section (e.g., *Cyn.* 3.6, 5.6). English translations: William Dansey, *Arrian on Coursing* (1831, on the Internet Archive and Project Gutenberg ebook 78013); D. B. Hull, *Hounds and Hunting in Ancient Greece* (1964); A. A. Phillips & M. M. Willcock, *Xenophon & Arrian: On Hunting* (1999). A useful older English rendering (with the Latin coursing epigraph from Ovid) is at Wikisource, "Cynegetica/Ancient Method of Coursing, from Arrian."

**(a) The vertragus — appearance, temperament, use:**
- **Etymology and identity (*Cyn.* 3.6):** "the swift Celtic dogs are called *ouertragoi* (οὐέρτραγοι) in the Celtic tongue, not from any country... but from their swiftness" (Greek quoted in the Loeb Grattius notes and Stadter's GRBS study). Arrian carefully argues, against Xenophon, that Xenophon simply did not know these dogs.
- **Correcting Xenophon:** Where Xenophon said hares are caught "contrary to nature, by accident," Arrian retorts that with a well-bred, spirited vertragus a hare can only escape through broken ground, thickets, ditches, or being startled too close.
- **Appearance (*Cyn.* 4–6):** "Splendid animals, the best bred of them, with fine eyes, fine bodies all over, fine coats, and fine appearance." They should be long from head to tail, sturdily built, with a pointed muzzle and large soft ears; eyes prominent, large, bright, that "should astonish the man who sees them." Coat colour "makes no difference... not even if hounds are black or tan or white all over" (*Cyn.* 6.1) — again correcting Xenophon.
- **Care:** Arrian recommends hounds sleep with people, "as they become thereby affectionately attached — pleased with the contact of the human body."

**(b) Celtic hare-coursing and hunting on horseback:**
Arrian distinguishes two kinds of Celtic ("Gaulish") dog: rough, ugly scent hounds called **Segusii** (from a district of Gaul), comparable to Carian and Cretan dogs; and the swift **vertragi**. He describes **four Celtic modes of coursing**: (1) wealthy Gauls send out hare-finders at dawn, then course on horseback; (2) less wealthy amateurs beat the ground in a mounted line; (3) true enthusiasts on foot; (4) mixing scent-finders with sighthounds, the sighthounds serving "the use of Xenophon's nets." Crucially, the point of the sport is **not the kill**: "one does not take hounds out in order to catch the beast, but for a race and competition, at least if one is a true sportsman." Arrian recounts personally dismounting to save the hare and even striking dogs that killed a good hare. He gives coursing cries by name: "Well done, Cirrha! Well done, Bonna! There's a good Hormé!"

**(c) His own dog Hormé:** Arrian names his own gray-eyed vertragus **Hormé** (Ὁρμή, "Impulse"), calling her "most swift and wise and divine" — using ἱερωτάτη, a superlative of ἱερός ("most sacred/divine"; M. M. Willcock renders it "out of this world").

**(d) Does Arrian locate any of this in Hispania/Iberia?** **No.** Throughout the *Cynegeticus*, coursing and the vertragus are located among the **Celts/Gauls**, never in Spain. The association of the vertragus with Iberia is a **later conflation** driven by (i) the etymology *Canis Gallicus* → Spanish *galgo*, and (ii) the separate (and probably genuine) fact that Arrian governed Baetica.

**(e) CRITICAL: Did Arrian govern Baetica? — verified, not debunked.**
The claim is **well-supported in modern scholarship**, contrary to the "likely false" framing:
- The evidence is a **Greek verse inscription to Artemis discovered at Córdoba (Corduba) in 1968**, dedicated by "Arrianus, proconsul" (*Arrianos anthupatos*), offering the goddess verse "better than gold and silver" and preferable to "the spoils of the chase." It was first published by **A. Tovar, "Un nuevo epigrama griego de Córdoba," in *Estudios sobre la obra de Américo Castro* (Madrid, 1971), pp. 401–412** (with photograph between pp. 408–409), and re-published as A. Tovar, "Un nuevo epigrama griego: ¿Arriano de Nicomedia, procónsul de Bética?", *Archivo Español de Arqueología* 48 (1975): 167–173.
- **Ronald Syme** ("The Career of Arrian," *HSCP* 86, 1982) writes verbatim: "At Corduba a proconsul of Baetica called Arrianus consecrates a Greek poem to Artemis, declaring his gift better than gold and silver, better than the products of hunting. None other than the author of the *Cynegetica*, the notion is highly attractive." He reconstructs sortition for the proconsulate c. 123–124 CE.
- **A. B. Bosworth**, "Arrian in Baetica" (*GRBS* 17, 1976), examines the inscription in detail and concludes his reconstruction "is both supported by the extant work of the historian Arrian and which, if correct, is only compatible with his authorship of the epigram" — i.e., the offering of verse instead of hunting spoils fits the philosopher-huntsman precisely.
- **Philip A. Stadter** (*Arrian of Nicomedia*, 1980) and the standard reference works accept the Baetican proconsulate (c. 123–126 CE) as part of Arrian's *cursus*: adlection to the Senate under Hadrian → legionary legate on the Danube → **proconsul of Baetica (unarmed senatorial province) c. 123–126** → suffect consul 129/130 (first Bithynian consul) → **legatus Augusti of Cappadocia 131–137** (his major military command, where he repelled the Alani) → archon of Athens 145/6 → retirement.
- **Caveats to flag:** (i) A minority (e.g., M. Marcovich, *ZPE* 12, 1973) doubted the identification. (ii) The Spanish study "Arriano de Nicomedia y la Bética, de nuevo" concludes, from formal analysis of the support (a marble altar of Almadén de la Plata), that "the piece was made at the beginning of the 3rd c. CE, so that it is either a later copy of the original monument, or else the dedicant was not Arrian of Nicomedia." (iii) Even granting the governorship, **the inscription says nothing about hunting hares in Spain**; the popular claim that Arrian "first encountered the vertragus in Baetica" traces to the Dog Law Reporter blog ("Anthropomorphism in Antiquity," May 2011), which reports that "Richard Hawkins suggests he may have first encountered the vertragus" there — this is **speculation, not fact**, and the *Cynegeticus* itself frames the vertragus as Celtic/Gaulish. **Label: proconsulship = scholarly consensus (with a copy-date caveat); "encountered the vertragus in Spain / hunted hares in Spain" = traditional claim, unverified.**

### 5. Pliny the Elder, *Naturalis Historia* (77 CE)
Book VIII is the zoological book. Standard citation by book and paragraph (Loeb: Rackham; also Perseus, Bostock & Riley; attalus.org). Dog material clusters at **VIII.61 (40)** "The qualities of the dog; examples of its attachment to its master; nations which have kept dogs for war," **VIII.62** "The generation of the dog," and **VIII.63** "Remedies against canine madness."
- **Hunting sagacity (VIII.147):** "in hunting... their skill and sagacity is most outstanding. A hound traces and follows footprints... how silent and secret but how significant an indication is given first by the tail and then by the muzzle."
- **Cross-breeding (VIII.148):** "The Indians want hounds to be sired by tigers... Similarly the Gauls breed hounds from wolves; each of their packs has one of the dogs as leader and guide; the pack accompanies this leader in the hunt and pays it obedience."
- **Iberia:** Book VIII.81 (55) discusses the different species of hares and the Spanish/Balearic rabbit plague (the word *cuniculus* is of Iberian origin), but Pliny gives no distinctively Iberian dog breed. He famously calls the dog among the most faithful of animals to man.

### 6. Varro, *De Re Rustica* (c. 37 BCE)
Varro (116–27 BCE) discusses dogs at **II.9**, dealing **only with the guard/shepherd dog** (*canis pastoralis*), not the hunting dog. He recognizes the two functional sorts — "the hunting-dog suited to chase the beasts of the forest, and the other which is procured as a watch-dog" (II.9.2) — but treats only the latter: large head, drooping ears, thick neck, wide paws, deep bark, white coat (to distinguish it from a wolf in the dark), fitted with a nail-studded collar (*melium*) (II.9.5, 15). He advises buying from a shepherd, not a hunter, so it follows sheep rather than chasing a hare or stag; "no farm is safe without them" (I.19.3). Text at LacusCurtius and Perseus (Hooper/Ash Loeb, 1934).

### 7. Columella, *De Re Rustica* Book VII (c. 60–65 CE)
Columella (L. Iunius Moderatus Columella, from Gades in Baetica) treats dogs at **VII.12**, immediately after sheep, goats, and pigs. He explicitly **excludes the hunting dog** because it "not only does not help the farmer but actually lures him away from his work, and makes him lazy about it" (VII.11.2 / VII.12.1). He distinguishes:
- the **farm-yard guard dog** (*villaticus*): heavily built, large head, drooping ears, black coat (to frighten thieves by day, be invisible by night);
- the **shepherd dog** (*pastoralis*): white (to distinguish it from a wolf at dawn/dusk), long and slim, strong and fast enough to repel or pursue a wolf.
He praises the dog's loyalty (VII.12.1: "*Quis famulus amantior domini? Quis fidelior comes? Quis custos incorruptior? Quis excubitor inveniri potest vigilantior?*") and recommends two-syllable names (VII.12.13), giving Greek and Latin examples — *Scylax/Ferox, Lacon/Celer* (male); *Spudé, Alké, Rhomé / Lupa, Cerva, Tigris* (female). Text at LacusCurtius (Forster/Heffner Loeb).

### 8. Oppian / Pseudo-Oppian, *Cynegetica* (Greek, after 212 CE)
The *Cynegetica* attributed to "Oppian" is now generally assigned to **Pseudo-Oppian** (Oppian of Apamea), distinct from the Oppian of Anazarbus who wrote the *Halieutica*; it is dedicated to Caracalla (Antoninus, son of Severus and Julia Domna), placing it after 212 CE. Standard citation by book and line; Loeb (A. W. Mair, 1928) and LacusCurtius/ToposText (topostext.org/work/525) carry the text.
- **Breed catalogue (I.368–538):** "Those breeds are without number" (I.400). At I.393–400 Oppian gives cross-breeding pairs: "Mate Arcadian with Elean, Cretan with Paeonian, Carian with Thracian, Tuscan breed with Laconian; **put a Sarmatian sire with an Iberian dam**. So shall you mix the breeds aright; but far best of all it is that the breeds should remain pure." This is a direct mention of an **Iberian bitch** in a breeding scheme.
- **Laconian:** recommended "for the swift chase of gazelle and deer and swift-footed hare" (I.412ff).
- **Molossian:** "Impetuous and of steadfast valour, who attack even bearded bulls... They are not swift, but they have abundant spirit and genuine strength unspeakable and dauntless courage" (I.414ff).
- **Colour for hunting dogs:** best are those coloured like wolves, tigers, foxes, leopards, or "Demeter's yellow corn" (I.430ff); trackers should be silent (I.449ff).

### 9. Nemesianus, *Cynegetica* (283/284 CE)
M. Aurelius Olympius Nemesianus of Carthage wrote his *Cynegetica* under Carinus and Numerian; c. 325 hexameters survive (incomplete). Standard citation by line; Loeb (Duff & Duff, *Minor Latin Poets*, 1934) and LacusCurtius (penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Nemesianus).
- **Breed origins (lines 224ff):** "But it is not only Spartan whelps or only Molossian which you must rear: sundered Britain sends us a swift sort, adapted to hunting-tasks in our world. You should not disdain the pedigree of the **Pannonian** breed, **nor those whose progeny springs from Spanish blood**. Moreover, keen whelps are produced within the confines of dry Libya... Besides, Tuscan dogs often give a satisfaction not foreign to us." — This is the **most explicit classical mention of dogs of Spanish stock** in a hunting-breed context, though Nemesianus lists Spain alongside Britain, Pannonia, Libya, and Tuscany without singling it out.
- **Breeding/whelp selection (lines 103ff; and later, ~line 497):** detailed advice on mating, and the notorious "ring of fire" test where the mother is set to rescue her best puppies first.

### 10. Roman Iberian archaeology — hunting dogs and coursing scenes
The archaeological record of Roman Hispania (3rd–4th c. CE, the Late Empire) contains numerous cynegetic (hunting) mosaics and some frescoes. The comprehensive scholarly syntheses are **Guadalupe López Monteagudo, "La caza en el mosaico romano. Iconografía y simbolismo,"** *Antigüedad y Cristianismo* 8 (1991): 497–512 (open access at digital.csic.es, handle 10261/16509); **Milagros Guardia Pons, *Los mosaicos de la Antigüedad tardía en Hispania* (PPU, Barcelona, 1992)**, esp. pp. 327–335; **P. Argüelles Álvarez & D. Piay Augusto, "Escenas cinegéticas en los mosaicos de las villae hispanas y el tratamiento de la caza en las fuentes literarias"** (Gredos-USAL, handle 10366/149594); and **M. T. Caetano, *Animalia quae lacte aluntur: Mamíferos nos Mosaicos Romanos da Península Ibérica* (Lisbon, 2017)**.

- **Interpretive frame:** López Monteagudo (1991) argues the hunt mosaics belong to the aristocratic "cycles of the latifundia," exalting the *virtus* and social standing of the *dominus*; the iconography derives from North African mosaics and Hellenistic painting, not a Roman invention. The dominant subject is the **big-game hunt (venatio)** — boar, felines, deer — not hare-coursing.
- **La Olmeda (Pedrosa de la Vega, Palencia; Tarraconensis), 4th c.:** The great *oecus* hunt mosaic (below the Achilles-at-Skyros scene) is a big-game/venatio composition — lions, leopards, panther, bear, tigress, deer, and **a wild boar cornered by a pack of dogs** ("diversos perros acorralando a un jabalí"). The dogs are a generic hunting **pack (jauría)**, not gracile coursing sighthounds. (Pedro de Palol, *La villa romana de La Olmeda... Guía de las excavaciones*, 1982; Abásolo & Martínez 2012: 33–44; J. A. Abásolo, *Los mosaicos de La Olmeda*, 2013.) **Note: a popularly repeated "leaping galgo pursuing a hare at La Olmeda" is *not* found in the scholarly descriptions and should be treated as unverified.**
- **Mérida (Emerita Augusta; Lusitania):**
  - *Casa de la calle Suárez Somonte* fresco (4th c., Museo Nacional de Arte Romano): a hunting panel showing "la caza de la liebre, que es apresada por un lebrel ante la atenta mirada del cazador a caballo" — a **hare caught by a coursing hound (*lebrel*) watched by a mounted hunter** (Guardia Pons 1992: 327–335). This is the clearest Hispano-Roman coursing/sighthound image.
  - "Avra" mosaic (Asamblea de Extremadura): a **named galgo, "AVRA," that has already caught a hare** (F. Palma García, *Mérida, excavaciones arqueológicas* 8, 2002: 159–208).
  - *Villa de las Tiendas / El Hinojal*: mid-4th c. boar-hunt and feline-hunt mosaics with mounted hunters in Constantinian dress (López Monteagudo 1991; Álvarez Martínez).
- **Córdoba (Corduba; Baetica):** the *Mosaico de Thalassius* (4th c.) with the inscription *THALASSIUS QUI VENATOR* shows a mounted hunter whose **named galgos "NIMBUS" and "LATERAS"** course a fleeing hare (López Monteagudo et al. 1999) — a named-sighthound coursing scene in Baetica.
- **Conímbriga (Lusitania):** the *Mosaico do Auriga* (3rd c.), *Casa dos Repuxos/Surtidores*: a seasonal panel where "unos galgos persiguen la presa" ("galgos pursue the prey") with mounted hunters (López Monteagudo 1990, 1999).
- **Itálica (Baetica):** the (now lost) *Mosaico de Galatea* showed deer pursued by dogs in woodland (Blanco Freijeiro 1978); the dogs are described generically.
- **Centcelles (Tarragona; Tarraconensis):** the cupola mausoleum mosaic (late 4th–early 5th c.) has a stag/antelope venatio with the *dominus*, hunters, and dogs, but the dogs are not the emphasized element (A. Arbeiter, *Butlletí Arqueològic* 5.32, 2009: 671–684).

**Assessment:** Spanish and Portuguese scholarship uses "galgo" and "lebrel" **descriptively** for the gracile coursing sighthounds that genuinely do appear (Conímbriga, Mérida, Córdoba). So gracile sighthound-type dogs coursing hares **are attested in Hispano-Roman art**. But the big-game pack hound and molossian-type dominate the venatio mosaics, and — decisively — **no peer-reviewed archaeologist identifies these depicted dogs with the modern breed "Galgo Español."** That breed-continuity link appears only in kennel-club and popular literature, and rests on textual argument (Arrian, and the *Canis Gallicus* → *galgo* etymology), not on art-historical breed identification.

### 11. The "Codex Romanicus" — a non-existent text
There is **no Roman legal, encyclopedic, or cynegetic text known to scholarship as the "Codex Romanicus"** dealing with hunting dogs or territorial administration. The label appears to be a garbled popular conflation. The most likely real referents being misremembered are:
- The **Cynegetica/Cynegeticus tradition** (Xenophon, Grattius, Arrian, Oppian, Nemesianus) — the actual body of ancient "hunting-dog books."
- **Pliny's *Naturalis Historia*** — the great Roman encyclopedic compendium that includes the dog material.
- The genuine late Roman **legal codices** whose names are superficially similar: the *Codex Gregorianus* (c. 291–294 CE), *Codex Hermogenianus*, *Codex Theodosianus* (438 CE), and *Codex Justinianus* (529–534 CE). These postdate the Greece/Rome period covered here and concern law, not hunting dogs, but their fame plausibly explains the "codex" element of the confusion.
- Bibliographically, "Codex Romanus" is a real term but denotes unrelated manuscripts — the *Codex Vaticanus Ottobonianus Latinus 1829* (a Catullus manuscript, siglum "R") and the *Vergilius Romanus* (an illustrated Virgil, c. 500 CE). Neither concerns dogs or administration.

**Verdict:** *"Codex Romanicus" as a Roman authority on hunting dogs or territory = does not exist; traditional/popular reference, unverified and almost certainly a conflation.*

## Recommendations
1. **Write the Arrian article as the batch centerpiece**, built on the Roos text and the Phillips–Willcock and Dansey translations, with exact section citations (*Cyn.* 3.6 for the etymology, chs. 4–6 for the physical description, the coursing chapters, and the closing for Hormé). Frame the vertragus explicitly as **Celtic/Gaulish**, and treat the Iberian association as a later inference.
2. **Handle the Baetica question with precision**: state affirmatively that modern prosopography (Syme 1982; Bosworth 1976; Stadter 1980) accepts Arrian's proconsulship of Baetica c. 123–126 CE on the basis of the Córdoba Artemis inscription, but flag (a) the stone's possible early-3rd-c. date / copy status (per "Arriano de Nicomedia y la Bética, de nuevo"), and (b) that the "he saw the vertragus in Spain / hunted hares in Spain" claim is **not in Arrian and is unverified** (traceable to breed writer Richard Hawkins via the Dog Law Reporter blog). This is the single most important accuracy point in the batch.
3. **Quote primary Latin/Greek verbatim** where digitized: Martial XIV.200 (Heraeus/Perseus/Loeb), Grattius 156 and 202–208 (LacusCurtius/Loeb), Nemesianus 224ff (LacusCurtius/Loeb), Oppian I.393–400 (ToposText/Loeb), Arrian 3.6 (Greek in Stadter/Bosworth, Duke GRBS). Link Perseus/LacusCurtius/Loeb URLs for each.
4. **For the archaeology article, foreground López Monteagudo 1991 and Guardia Pons 1992**, distinguish the venatio big-game mosaics (La Olmeda) from the genuine coursing scenes (Conímbriga Auriga, Mérida Suárez Somonte fresco and "Avra" mosaic, Córdoba Thalassius), and state plainly that the modern-breed identification is popular, not scholarly.
5. **Kill the "Codex Romanicus" thread** cleanly with a short debunking note pointing readers to the real Cynegetica tradition and the late legal codices.
6. **Benchmarks that would change these conclusions:** discovery of (a) an ancient text explicitly siting the vertragus/coursing in Hispania, (b) a securely dated peer-reviewed identification of a Hispano-Roman mosaic dog as morphologically Galgo-specific, or (c) new epigraphy overturning the Baetica proconsorship.

## Caveats
- **No unbroken lineage.** Nothing in the Greek/Roman sources establishes descent from any Roman-era dog to the modern Galgo Español. The literary trail is about the *vertragus* (a Celtic sighthound) and about coursing as a practice; the modern breed link is a reasonable-sounding but unproven inference from breed literature. Label such claims "traditional claim, unverified."
- **The vertragus is Celtic/Gaulish in the sources, not Iberian.** The *Canis Gallicus* → Spanish *galgo* etymology explains the later Spanish association but does not prove Roman-Iberian origin.
- **Arrian's Baetica governorship is accepted, not debunked** — the opposite of the flagged expectation — but the inscription (a) may survive only in an early-3rd-c. copy and (b) contains no hunting-hare-in-Spain content.
- **Authorship notes:** Xenophon's *Cynegeticus* has a disputed genuineness (the "pseudo-Xenophon" question), though Arrian accepted it as authentic; the *Cynegetica* under "Oppian" is by Pseudo-Oppian (Oppian of Apamea), not the *Halieutica* poet.
- **Source hygiene:** breed-club and generic web pages frequently repeat the "Arrian governed Baetica and there met the Galgo's ancestor" story as settled fact; only the governorship is scholarly, and the rest is embellishment. The strongest primary-text access points are Perseus (perseus.tufts.edu), LacusCurtius (penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer), the Loeb Classical Library (loebclassics.com), ToposText, and the *GRBS* articles by Bosworth and Stadter.
