# Batch 06: Medieval Fueros and Law — Galgo Español Research Corpus (11th–15th Centuries)

## TL;DR
- The famous "Fuero de Salamanca galgo provision" is **real but badly mythologized**: the charter does contain an article penalizing the killing of a galgo, but the widely repeated figure of "300 sueldos" is unsupported by any critical edition (the actual penalty is 2 maravedís), and the "9th-century" dating is a myth — the text is a 12th–13th-century compilation not given definitive form until the second half of the 13th century.
- Medieval Iberian hunting-dog law is genuinely rich and well documented: the Cuenca–Teruel and Coria–Cáceres–Usagre fuero families (12th–13th c.) contain explicit, verbatim galgo provisions (fines of 5 aureos in Cuenca, 20 sueldos in Teruel, 2 maravedís in Usagre/Cáceres/Coria), and Alfonso X's Siete Partidas (13th c.) and Alfonso XI's Libro de la montería (14th c.) confirm hunting and hunting-dog husbandry as codified, elite-associated practice.
- Continuity of hare-coursing with sighthounds from Roman Hispania (Arrian, 2nd c.) through the medieval fueros to the present is **plausible in practice but weakly documented as an unbroken institutional chain**; the term "galgo" (from *canis gallicus*) is securely attested from the late 11th century ("galgo leporario," texts of 1073).

## Key Findings

**1. Fuero de Salamanca "galgo" provision — Traditional claim, partially confirmed, headline figure disproven.**
The charter does contain a provision listing the galgo among protected dogs, with the working text: *"Todo omne que matar perro de ganado, o podengo, o galgo, peche dos maravedís, e por caravo, cinco sueldos"* ("Any man who kills a herding dog, or a podengo, or a galgo, shall pay two maravedís, and for a caravo, five sueldos"). The commonly cited "300 sueldos" fine matches no primary edition and is **Disproven** — the verifiable penalty is **2 maravedís**. The "9th-century" dating popular on breed websites is **Disproven**; scholarly consensus (José Luis Martín Martín & Javier Coca, *Fuero de Salamanca*, Diputación de Salamanca, 1987, pp. 15–18) dates the materials to the 12th century with definitive configuration in the second half of the 13th century.

**2. Siete Partidas (Alfonso X, compiled c. 1256–1265).**
Partida II, Título V, Ley XX ("Cómo el rey debe ser mañoso en cazar") frames hunting as a virtue of kingship: *"Mañoso debe el rey ser et sabidor de otras cosas que se tornan en sabor et en alegría para poder mejor sofrir los grandes trabajos... una de las cosas que fallaron los antiguos que mas tiene pro es la caza"* — good for health, for relieving *pensamientos et saña* (cares and anger), and as training analogous to war. Partida I contains a law restricting clergy: *"Venadores ni cazadores no deben ser los clérigos... ni deben tener azores ni halcones, ni canes para cazar"* — clerics may not keep hawks or hunting dogs, though they may fish and snare. The Partidas treat hunting as socially stratified (a royal/noble pursuit) but the searches did **not** surface an explicit occurrence of the word "galgo/galga" in the Partidas hunting/coursing laws themselves (negative result — see Caveats).

**3. Other fueros — Confirmed, verbatim galgo provisions.**
- **Fuero de Cuenca** (c. 1189, rúbrica "De canibus," Ureña ed. 1935, pp. 712–715): *"quien mata alano, o sabueso, o galgo para 5 mrs., 'alanum, vel sabusum, vel galgum', 5 aureos ... e quien podenco ageno matare, peche diez mencalas ... e quien cravo, que albollon pueda entrar e sallir, matare, peche çinco mencales."* Britannica's "Fuero" entry confirms: *"by the end of the 12th century they tended to be complicated documents. That of Cuenca (c. 1189) is the most elaborate of the Castilian fueros."*
- **Fuero de Teruel** (ed. Gorosch, p. 347, 13th c.): *"Encara mando que qualquier que alán, o saueso, o galgo ageno matare o liçençiare ... peche XX sueldos; e por podenco peche XV sueldos, e por caravo peche X sueldos. E por todos los otros canes, quien los matare ... peche V sueldos."*
- **Fuero de Usagre** (13th c., Ureña & Bonilla ed. 1907, §§104–105): *"Qui matare galgo, o carauo, o can rostro, por el galgo pectet II morauetis domino suo... Et si al galgo quebrantare pierna al tanto, pectet como si lo matasse"* — breaking a galgo's leg carries the same penalty as killing it.
- **Fuero de Zorita de los Canes** (c. 1218–1250, Ureña ed. 1911): *"Tod aquel que alano, o sabueso, o galgo matare, peche V marauedis, si con testigos vençido fuere"*; also covers wounding (*firiere*) any *alano, sabueso, galgo, perro oueiero, o podenco*.
- **Fuero General de Navarra** (13th c., Ilarregui ed., lib. V, tít. VII, cap. XVIII): theft of *"galgo que caza, que traye correya al pescueza, con sortiella"*; stealing an *alano* or *galgo que caza* = 60 sueldos.
- **Fuero de Coria** (Alfonso VII, mid-12th c.): *"Qui matare galgo, o caravo, o can rastrero, por galgo peche dos maravedis a su duenno, e por caravo un maravedi a su duenno; e por rastrero un maravedi... e por podenco o alano dos maravedis a su duenno, si a tuerto los matare."* The **Fuero de Cáceres** (Alfonso VIII, 13th c.) carries the parallel *"por el galgo pecte II morabetis domino suo."*

**4. Hunting treatises.**
Alfonso XI's *Libro de la montería* (mid-14th c.; the surviving text dates to the era of Pedro I with 16th-c. additions) is the major Iberian hunting manual, describing dog breeds (alanos, sabuesos), their breeding, diseases and cures, and the montes where royal hunts took place — continuing a tradition begun under Alfonso X. Its authorship was debated for six centuries (surveyed by José Manuel Fradejas Rueda); manuscripts attribute it only to "el rey don Alfonso," with tradition settling on Alfonso XI. The 15th-century anonymous *Tratado de la montería* likewise describes hunting dogs and cross-breeding. Crucially, these are **big-game (montería) manuals** — boar, bear, deer — distinct from the *cacería de liebre con galgo* (hare-coursing) that defines the galgo. The etymology of "galgo" (from Latin *canis gallicus*, "Gallic/Celtic dog," via *galgus*) is well attested; *"galgo leporario"* ("the one that chases hares," later *lebrel*) appears in texts of **1073**, and the Cartulario de Eslonza records a bequest of "Urso galgo colore nigro" dated 1081.

**5. Social class and coursing.**
Hare-coursing with sighthounds was historically associated with landed and noble classes across Europe, but the fuero evidence shows galgos owned and legally protected across frontier *concejo* society (*caballeros villanos* and commoners alike). The often-repeated claim that Arrian (2nd c.) described Hispanic hare-coursing as practiced "without distinction of social class" is a **breed-literature paraphrase that inverts the source**: Arrian's *Cynegeticus* (Dansey trans., *Arrian on Coursing*) actually distinguishes a "superior class of Celtic gentlemen" who employed others to find hares, a "less opulent" second class, and "a third class [who] sallied forth on foot... really workmen at the sport." Treat the "no class distinction" gloss as **Unsupported**.

## Details

### Fuero de Salamanca — anatomy of a myth
Breed-history websites (mascotasderaza.com, caninacostadelsol.es, xn--galgoespaol-9db.com and others) uniformly assert a "Fuero de Salamanca (siglo IX)" imposing "300 sueldos" for killing/stealing a galgo. Both elements fail verification:

- **Dating.** The Fuero de Salamanca is a composite "fuero extenso" in Leonese romance. Martín Martín & Coca (1987) show it accreted from the early 12th century (materials attributable to Alfonso VII, Fernando II, Alfonso IX) to the second half of the 13th century. CORDE dates the reference Castro & Onís text to c. 1300; the three manuscripts range from the 13th century (Escorial g.IV.19) to the 14th (Salamanca town archive, the most Castilianized, edited by Martín & Coca). The "9th century" label conflates the fuero with the 9th-century repopulation of the Duero valley described only as background in Julián Sánchez Ruano's 1870 preliminary discourse.
- **The provision.** The reference edition is Américo Castro & Federico de Onís, *Fueros leoneses de Zamora, Salamanca, Ledesma y Alba de Tormes* (1916); Manuel Alvar's *El fuero de Salamanca: lingüística e historia* (CSIC/Univ. Granada, 1968, digitized at Biblioteca Virtual Miguel de Cervantes) is the linguistic study. The galgo appears lumped with herding dogs and podencos at a fine of **2 maravedís** (with 5 sueldos for a caravo). The "300 sueldos" figure could not be traced to any primary edition and appears to be an unsourced popular error; comparable fueros (Coria, Cáceres, Usagre) give 2 maravedís, Teruel 20 sueldos, Cuenca 5 aureos — none approaching 300.

**OCR/citation-uncertainty note:** The precise article number sometimes given as "210" derives from a secondary reproduction (jralonso.es) and could not be confirmed against a scholarly concordance in the available sources. Treat the **wording** as reliable but the **article number** as uncertain pending a concordance check.

### Siete Partidas
The Partidas survive in the Real Academia de la Historia edition (1807, digitized at Cervantes Virtual and the Biblioteca Digital de Castilla y León; Partida II is at Tomo II). Partida II Tít. V Ley XX is the locus classicus for royal hunting as statecraft. The attribution/dating debate — sparked by Alfonso García-Gallo's 1951–52 article arguing the Partidas were a 14th-century reworking of the Espéculo rather than a finished work of Alfonso X's reign — bears on how one dates any single Partidas provision, and should be footnoted whenever a Partidas law is cited as "13th century."

### Fuero de Cuenca family and the "De canibus" rubric
The Cuenca fuero's rubric "De canibus" penalizes killing named hunting dogs and also references *canem rusticum* and herding dogs. The Zorita, Alcázar (romanceado), Teruel, Usagre and Cáceres texts belong to the same legal family — meaning the "galgo protection" tradition is genuinely a **Cuenca–Teruel/Extremaduran phenomenon of the 12th–13th centuries**, not a 9th-century Salamancan innovation. Nearly 800 fueros were granted between the 11th and 14th centuries (Britannica), which is why breed literature can list a long roster (Zorita, Cáceres, Alcalá, Usagre, Úbeda, Ledesma, Alba de Tormes, Cuenca, Béjar, Sepúlveda, Alcaraz, Salamanca, Plasencia, Alarcón, Baeza, Teruel, etc.) — but most entries on that roster trace to the same two legal families.

### Fuero Real and Fuero de Sepúlveda
The Fuero Real of Alfonso X (promulgated c. 1255; 4 books, 72 titles, 550 laws; ed. Martínez Díez 1988, and BOE/Pérez Martín 2018) is structured Book I (church/king/vassals), II (courts), III (civil law), IV (penal/mercantile). No specific galgo/coursing provision surfaced in the sources consulted (negative result). The Fuero de Sepúlveda (Latin "breve" c. 1076, extenso in romance, 14th c.) is the subject of Rafael de Floranes's 18th-century *Aves de caza: anotaciones al Fuero de Sepúlveda*, indicating hunting-bird (not specifically galgo) content; this is a lead for a future batch rather than a confirmed galgo source.

## Recommendations
1. **Record the Salamanca claim as split-classification:** (a) galgo provision exists — **Confirmed**; (b) "300 sueldos" figure — **Disproven**; (c) "9th century" date — **Disproven**; (d) correct penalty (2 maravedís) and date (12th–13th c.) — **Probable→Confirmed** pending a concordance check.
2. **Anchor the galgo-law narrative on the Cuenca–Teruel and Coria–Cáceres–Usagre families**, which have secure, verbatim, edition-backed provisions, rather than on Salamanca. These are your strongest primary-text assets.
3. **Commission a concordance check** in Elena Alvar, *Vocabulario del Fuero de Salamanca* (1982) or Carrasco & Carrasco, *Concordancias lematizadas* (1997), to pin the exact article number and folio for the Salamanca galgo article — the one remaining citation-grade gap. **Threshold to upgrade to "Confirmed" with citation:** a scholarly edition article number matching the quoted wording.
4. **Treat "continuity 8th–15th c." as Plausible, not Confirmed.** Upgrade only if a continuous chain of *coursing-specific* references (not just isolated fuero fines) can be assembled across the gap between Arrian (2nd c.) and the 11th-century "galgo leporario" attestation.
5. **Purge the Arrian "no class distinction" claim** from any corpus narrative, or re-label it as a modern paraphrase contradicted by Arrian's own text.

## Caveats
- The University of Murcia *Vocabulario del comercio medieval* (José Miguel Gual López) is the single richest secondary aggregator used here; its quotations are transcribed from named scholarly editions (Ureña, Gorosch, Ilarregui) and are reliable, but citations should always be traced to the underlying edition.
- Breed-history and pet-industry websites are unreliable on dates and figures (the "300 sueldos" and "9th century" errors originate there) and were used only to identify claims, never to confirm them.
- No live CORDE or full-text Cervantes Virtual/archive.org query could be executed for the Salamanca text in this batch; the galgo wording rests on scholarly-edition apparatus plus multiple secondary reproductions. The Salamanca article number remains the one open citation-grade item.
- Currency units vary across fueros (maravedís, aureos, sueldos, mencales) and are not directly comparable without regional/temporal conversion.
- The Siete Partidas' own dating is contested (García-Gallo debate); any Partidas provision cited as "13th century" should carry that qualification.
- The Arrian observation is a Roman-era source and, even read correctly, should not be projected onto medieval Iberian social structure without qualification.
