# Batch 01 — Deep Prehistory and Ancient Sighthound Context: A Source-Verified Research Pass for the Galgo Español Project

## TL;DR
- **Dog domestication is settled as a Late Pleistocene event from a now-extinct wolf population, but *where* and *how many times* remain genuinely unresolved.** Two brand-new *Nature* papers (25 March 2026) push the oldest genome-confirmed dogs back to ~15,800 years ago (Pınarbaşı, Türkiye) and ~14,200 years ago (Kesslerloch, Switzerland), yet their authors explicitly state genetics "cannot resolve where in the world, and how many times, dogs were domesticated."
- **Iberia has genuine, well-documented deep-prehistory dog evidence** — the ~17,000-year-old Erralla humerus (Basque Country), Mesolithic dogs at the Muge and Sado shell-middens (Portugal, ~7,600–7,900 cal BP), and abundant Neolithic–Chalcolithic–Bronze Age burials — but **no published osteometric or genetic study classifies any prehistoric Iberian dog as a "sighthound" or specifically Galgo-like.** Neolithic dogs are of "homogeneous sizes, between forty and fifty centimetres high" (Albizuri et al. 2019) and Copper Age dogs are eumetric/medium-sized (~0.50 m withers; Daza 2017); researchers deliberately avoid functional-type labels.
- **The "ancient sighthound" narrative is real but must be handled carefully.** Gracile coursing-type dogs appear in Egyptian and Saharan iconography from ~5,500–7,000 years ago, but peer-reviewed genomics (Zhang et al. 2023; Larson et al. 2012; Bergström et al. 2020) shows modern sighthounds are **polyphyletic**, arising independently in Africa, Europe, and the Middle East through **convergent human-driven selection** (implicating genes such as ESR1 and JAK2), not a single ancient lineage — and no scholarly source connects them in an unbroken line to the Galgo Español.

---

## Key Findings

1. **Consensus on domestication:** Dogs were the first domesticated animal and the only large species domesticated during the Pleistocene, from an extinct wolf population (or populations). This is settled. Genome-confirmed dogs now date to the Upper Palaeolithic (~15,800 and ~14,200 years ago per the 2026 *Nature* papers), pushing the direct genetic record back roughly 5,000 years from the previous ~10,900-year-old benchmark (Veretye, Russia).

2. **Single vs. multiple origin is still actively debated.** Frantz et al. (2016) proposed a dual origin (East and West Eurasia); Bergström et al. (2020) favored a single origin from one extinct wolf population; Bergström et al. (2022) proposed dual *wolf* ancestry. The 2026 papers reject a *wholly separate* European Palaeolithic domestication but leave the number and location of domestication events explicitly unresolved.

3. **"Proto-dogs" older than ~15,000 years are contested.** The Razboinichya/Altai (~33,000 BP), Goyet (~36,000 BP), and Předmostí specimens are morphologically ambiguous; all such Pleistocene specimens tested to date carry wolf genomic signatures (per the 2025 Evin et al. cranial study and the 2026 papers).

4. **Iberian record:** Real and well-documented from the Magdalenian onward, with a notable claim of local pre-Neolithic wolf domestication (Pires et al. 2019), later partial replacement by incoming Neolithic dog lineages, and rich ritual/funerary deposition in the Neolithic–Bronze Age. Morphometric work (Daza Perea 2017; Blázquez-Orta et al. 2022, 2024) documents morphological *diversity* but does **not** identify sighthound types.

5. **Egyptian/Near Eastern iconography:** The gracile, prick-eared, curl-tailed "tesem" and later lop-eared Saluki/Sloughi types are securely attested in Predynastic and Dynastic Egyptian art (Hendrickx's scholarship is the rigorous source), but popular breed-lineage claims (Pharaoh Hound, Ibizan Hound as direct descendants) are refuted by genetics.

6. **Methodological limits are severe and well-documented:** distinguishing early dog from wolf skeletally is hard; "breed" is a 19th-century closed-studbook concept inapplicable to ancient "types"; iconography is conventionalized and symbolic, not photographic; and similar body plans arose by convergent selection.

## Details

### 1. Dog Domestication: Current Scientific Consensus

**What is settled.** Dogs derive from grey wolves (*Canis lupus*), were the first domesticated animal, and are the only large animal domesticated in the Pleistocene, before agriculture (Larson et al. 2012, PNAS; Clutton-Brock 2016; Bergström et al. 2020, *Science*). The 2020 study — **Bergström, A. et al., "Origins and genetic legacy of prehistoric dogs," *Science* 370:557–564 (2020), DOI 10.1126/science.aba9572** — sequenced 27 ancient dog genomes up to ~11,000 years old and found that by ~11,000 years ago there were already at least five distinct dog lineages with different ancestries, and that dogs derive from a single ancient extinct wolf population — favoring (but not proving) a single origin. *Primary genetic study.*

**The single vs. multiple origin debate.**
- **Dual-origin hypothesis:** Frantz, L. A. F. et al., "Genomic and archaeological evidence suggest a dual origin of domestic dogs," *Science* 352:1228–1231 (2016), DOI 10.1126/science.aaf3161. Based on a 28× genome of a ~4,800-year-old Neolithic dog from Newgrange, Ireland, plus 59 ancient dog mtDNA sequences, they found a deep East Asian/Western Eurasian split dated ~14,000–6,400 years ago, and proposed independent domestication in each region, with East Asian dogs later partially replacing European Palaeolithic dogs. *Primary genetic study.*
- **Single-origin lean:** Bergström et al. 2020 (above).
- **Dual wolf ancestry:** Bergström, A. et al., "Grey wolf genomic history reveals a dual ancestry of dogs," *Nature* 607:313–320 (2022), DOI 10.1038/s41586-022-04824-9. Dogs derive most ancestry from an eastern Eurasian wolf source, but western/Near Eastern dogs show a second wolf ancestry component.
- **Siberian/LGM origin:** Perri, A. R. et al. (2021, PNAS) argued for domestication in Siberia during the Last Glacial Maximum.

**Newest evidence (2026).** Two back-to-back papers in *Nature* vol. 651 (25 March 2026), summarized in Callaway, E., "Who let the wolves in? Genetic record for domestic dogs pushed back by 5,000 years," *Nature* News, DOI 10.1038/d41586-026-00900-6, whose lede reads: "A pair of *Nature* papers published today have identified the oldest dog genomes on record in remains from archaeological sites spanning Europe and the Middle East."

- **Marsh, W. A. et al., "Dogs were widely distributed across western Eurasia during the Palaeolithic," *Nature* 651:995–1003 (2026), DOI 10.1038/s41586-026-10170-x** (senior author L. A. F. Frantz; first authors W. A. Marsh, Natural History Museum London, and L. Scarsbrook). They generated nuclear and mitochondrial genomes from 8 new individuals, the oldest being the **Pınarbaşı dog, Türkiye, ~15,800 cal BP** (radiocarbon 15,915–15,669 cal BP; 1.3× nuclear genome) and a **Gough's Cave dog, UK, ~14,300 cal BP**. Abstract: "a genetically homogeneous dog population was already widely distributed across Europe and Anatolia during the Late Upper Palaeolithic (by at least 14,300 years ago)," implying dogs were exchanged among "Magdalenian, Epigravettian and Anatolian hunter-gatherers." They identify "a major influx of eastern Eurasian dog ancestry during the Mesolithic," and define a new mtDNA clade "C5" (including Gough's Cave, Pınarbaşı, Bonn-Oberkassel, Kesslerloch, Grotta Paglicci). On Near Eastern wolf ancestry they favor gene flow over an independent domestication but concede the latter "remains possible." The Gough's Cave dog shows human-like postmortem skull modification, paralleling the site's human skull-cup treatment. *Primary genetic study.*
- **Bergström, A. et al., "Genomic history of early dogs in Europe," *Nature* 651:986–994 (2026), DOI 10.1038/s41586-026-10112-7** (senior author Pontus Skoglund, Francis Crick Institute; co-authors include Krause, Schuenemann, Larson, Frantz). They analysed 216 canid remains (181 Palaeolithic/Mesolithic European) using a new genome-wide capture array, distinguishing dog from wolf ancestry in 141. The oldest is a **14,200-year-old dog from Kesslerloch, Switzerland** (Magdalenian). Key quote: it "shares ancestry with later worldwide dogs—inconsistent with the hypothesis that European Upper Palaeolithic dogs derived wholly from a separate domestication process." They find a Neolithic influx of Southwest Asian dog ancestry into Europe "of smaller magnitude than in humans," so Mesolithic dogs contributed substantially to modern European dogs. Crucially: "our results cannot resolve where in the world, and how many times, dogs were domesticated." They re-dated a supposed Neolithic Swedish dog (Gökhem) to the Late Iron Age (~1.1 ka) and showed the Goyet (Belgium, 13.7 ka) canid has fully wolf-like ancestry. The Kesslerloch dog already shows the ~one-third reduction in heterozygosity relative to Late Glacial wolves, indicating domestication itself drove the diversity loss. *Primary genetic study.*

**Contested "proto-dogs."**
- **Razboinichya Cave, Altai, Siberia (~33,000 cal BP):** Ovodov, N. D. et al., "A 33,000-Year-Old Incipient Dog from the Altai Mountains of Siberia," *PLoS ONE* 6(7):e22821 (2011), DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0022821; aDNA follow-up in *PLoS ONE* 8(3):e57754 (2013). Described as an "incipient dog" that did not give rise to later lineages — domestication "disrupted by the LGM." *Primary, but genetically contested.*
- **Goyet, Belgium (~36,000 BP)** and **Předmostí, Czech Republic (~26,000–27,000 BP):** Germonpré et al. (2009, 2012) argued these are Palaeolithic dogs; Boudadi-Maligne & Escarguel (2014, *J. Archaeol. Sci.*) contested. Bergström et al. (2026) show the Goyet canid is wolf-like.
- **Bonn-Oberkassel, Germany (~14,200 BP):** the oldest secure human–dog joint burial. Janssens, L. et al., "A new look at an old dog: Bonn-Oberkassel reconsidered," *Journal of Archaeological Science* 92:126–138 (2018), DOI 10.1016/j.jas.2018.01.004. A late-juvenile dog (~27–28 weeks) buried with two adult humans; paleopathology indicates it survived canine distemper (contracted ~19 weeks) only with human care — evidence of a care/emotional bond; a second dog's molar was also present. *Primary archaeological report.*
- **Natufian Levant (~12,000–13,000 BP):** Tchernov, E. & Valla, F. F., "Two new dogs, and other Natufian dogs, from the southern Levant," *Journal of Archaeological Science* 24:65–95 (1997); Davis & Valla (1978, *Nature*) on the ʿAin Mallaha (Eynan) burial of an adult female with her hand resting on a 3–5-month-old puppy (~12,000 BP), and Hayonim Terrace burials. *Primary.*

**2025 cranial morphology study (methodological anchor):** Evin, A. et al., "The emergence and diversification of dog morphology," *Science* 390(6774):741–744 (13 Nov 2025), DOI 10.1126/science.adt0995. Using 3D geometric morphometrics on 643 canid crania spanning 50,000 years, a distinctive dog cranial morphology first appears ~11,000 cal BP, with substantial diversity already in early Holocene dogs — "many millennia before the intense human-mediated selection shaping modern dog breeds beginning in the 19th century." None of the 17 Late Pleistocene specimens (including some previously called dogs) had domestic morphology. Senior author Greger Larson (Oxford): "the first dogs continue to elude us." A Spanish-led eLetter (San-Juan-Nó et al.) responded, noting ~20 Pleistocene dogs including the Erralla dog exist.

### 2. Prehistoric Iberian Dogs

**Palaeolithic/Magdalenian.**
- **Erralla Cave (Zestoa, Gipuzkoa, Basque Country):** A canid humerus first identified as a dog by Altuna & Mariezkurrena (1985); recently directly dated and genetically confirmed — **Hervella, M. et al., "The domestic dog that lived ∼17,000 years ago in the Lower Magdalenian of Erralla site (Basque Country): A radiometric and genetic analysis," *Journal of Archaeological Science: Reports* 46:103706 (2022), DOI 10.1016/j.jasrep.2022.103706.** AMS-dated to 17,410–17,096 cal BP; genetically identified as *Canis lupus familiaris*; mtDNA haplogroup C, shared with other Magdalenian dogs; one of the oldest domestic dogs in Europe. The authors note this "raise[s] the possibility that wolf domestication occurred earlier than proposed until now, at least in western Europe." *Primary.*
- **Antón Koba (Gipuzkoa):** a dog dated ~13,250 BP (Arias's team); Cantabrian finds discussed in Altuna (1994).

**Mesolithic.**
- **Muge shell-middens (Cabeço da Arruda, Portugal):** Detry, C. & Cardoso, J. L., "On some remains of dog (*Canis familiaris*) from the Mesolithic shell-middens of Muge, Portugal," *Journal of Archaeological Science* 37 (2010), DOI 10.1016/j.jas.2010.06.013. An almost-complete dog without cut/burn marks, likely intentionally buried (not consumed). *Primary.*
- **Archaeogenetic synthesis:** **Pires, A. E. et al., "The curious case of the Mesolithic Iberian dogs: An archaeogenetic study," *Journal of Archaeological Science* 105:116–129 (2019), DOI 10.1016/j.jas.2019.02.002.** Six Canis remains from western Iberia directly dated 7,903–7,570 cal BP, identified as dogs via context, osteometry, and shared aquatic diet with humans; 37 additional Iberian dog remains (Neolithic–Late Antiquity) and two Palaeolithic + one Chalcolithic Canis (identified as wolves) analysed for comparison. They found a high frequency (83%) of dog mtDNA haplogroup A in Mesolithic Iberia — the first such report for pre-Neolithic Europe — arguing HgA "did not necessarily arrive in Europe from East Asia" and suggesting "pre-Neolithic local processes for Iberian wolf domestication." *Primary genetic study — a strong but contested claim (see Caveats).*
- **Poças de São Bento / Sado valley (Portugal):** a dog burial dated ~7,600 years, described as the oldest dog burial in southern Europe (Pablo Arias and team; reported via Agencia SINC), confirming Mesolithic hunter-gatherer dog inhumation in Iberia.

**Neolithic.**
- **Northeastern Iberian funerary dogs:** **Albizuri, S.; Nadal, J.; Martín, P.; Gibaja, J. F.; et al., "Dogs in funerary contexts during the Middle Neolithic in the northeastern Iberian Peninsula (5th–early 4th millennium BCE)," *Journal of Archaeological Science: Reports* 24:198–207 (2019)** (led by UAB and UB). 26 dogs from four sites/necropolises in the Barcelona region; isotopic analysis of 18. Per the University of Barcelona press release (15 Feb 2019), the dogs "were aged between one month and six years old… and had homogeneous sizes, between forty and fifty centimetres high," with diets including vegetable/carbohydrate input similar to humans. Ritual sacrifice/burial is associated with the Pit Grave (*sepulcros de fosa*) culture (4200–3600 BCE) and the practice persisted, in various forms, into the Iron Age. *Primary.*

**Chalcolithic/Copper Age and Bronze Age.**
- Principal sites: Camino de las Yeseras (San Fernando de Henares, Madrid), Las Matillas (Alcalá de Henares), La Huelga (Dueñas, Palencia), Can Roqueta and Torre Romeu (Sabadell, Barcelona), Minferri (Lleida), El Portalón (Sierra de Atapuerca, Burgos), Barrio del Castillo (Torrejón de Ardoz, Madrid).
- **Daza Perea, A., "Preliminary Studies of Late Prehistoric Dog (*Canis lupus f. familiaris* Linnaeus, 1758) Remains from the Iberian Peninsula: Osteometric and 2D Geometric Morphometric Approaches," *Papers from the Institute of Archaeology* 27(1): Art. 12 (2017), DOI 10.5334/pia-487** (open access). 53 mandibles (13 archaeological, 16 modern breeds); mean shoulder height ~44–47 cm; CVA revealed distinct morphological groups suggesting "unrecognized morphological variability," with a proposed typology by deposition context/function. As summarized by Grandal-d'Anglade, Albizuri & Nieto ("Who let the dogs in?", CSIC), Copper Age dogs are "well-proportioned, with a balanced appearance, an eumetric morphology, and a relatively homogeneous, medium size at a withers height of about 0.50 m." *Primary/synthesis.*
- **Blázquez-Orta, R. et al., "Dogs from the past: Exploring morphology in mandibles from Iberian archaeological sites using 3D geometric morphometrics," *Journal of Archaeological Science: Reports* (2024), DOI 10.1016/j.jasrep.2024.104616 (article S2352409X24002888).** Nine dog hemimandibles from Chalcolithic Barrio del Castillo and Bronze Age El Portalón, compared by 3D geometric morphometrics with modern Iberian wolves, dogs, and foxes; examines size continuity between Chalcolithic and Bronze Age.
- **Blázquez-Orta et al. (2022), "Morphological study of mandibular Canis remains from El Portalón (Sierra de Atapuerca) Holocene site," *Spanish Journal of Palaeontology* 37(1):53–68, DOI 10.7203/sjp.24124.**
- **"A multidisciplinary study of Iberian Chalcolithic dogs," *Journal of Archaeological Science: Reports* (2022), article S2352409X22000013:** characterizes Chalcolithic dogs (ca. 5000–4000 BP), confirming morphological diversity "was already present in such early times."
- **El Portalón dog consumption:** University of Burgos / CENIEH (Nohemí Sala): cut marks, intentional fractures, fire/cooking evidence, and probable human bite marks; dog consumption in Neolithic levels (7000–4500 BP) is among the oldest evidence of dog consumption in Iberia. The UBU statement emphasizes the osteological difficulty: dog and wolf bones show "hardly any morphological differences," so *size* is the main diagnostic (the early domestic dog being significantly smaller than the wolf).
- **Doctoral synthesis:** "Estudio arqueozoológico del perro en la Prehistoria Reciente de la Península Ibérica" (UAM institutional repository) compiles 160 deposits and studies material from 23 sites, concluding that placement of whole/partial dogs in structures was a symbolic practice from the end of the 4th millennium onward across the peninsula. *Spanish-language academic source.*

**Sighthound classification — the crucial negative finding.** Across all this Iberian literature, **no study classifies prehistoric Iberian dogs as sighthounds, greyhound-type, or specifically gracile/cursorial.** Sizes cluster ~40–50 cm at the withers (medium/small), and Horard-Herbin et al. (2014) explicitly place Late Glacial dogs of "the southwest and north of Spain" in the *small* category (30–45 cm or <30 cm), not the large coursing range. Researchers document morphological *diversity* without functional-type attribution; the Daza Perea typology is by deposition context, not breed-type. **Report this as a negative result: the claim that a Galgo-like sighthound existed in Iberian prehistory is not supported in the peer-reviewed archaeozoological record.**

### 3. Egypt, Near East, and Early Sighthound Iconography

**Predynastic Egypt — the rigorous scholarship is Stan Hendrickx's.**
- Hendrickx, S., "The dog, the *Lycaon pictus* and order over chaos in Predynastic Egypt," in Kroeper, Chłodnicki & Kobusiewicz (eds.), *Archaeology of Early Northeastern Africa* (Studies in African Archaeology 9), Poznań (2006): 723–749. Catalogs dogs on Predynastic "White Cross-lined" pottery (e.g., Naqada tomb 1644, Ashmolean 1895.482; a Moscow/Pushkin plate showing a hunter holding four dogs on leashes; a Torino plate with three dogs). Distinguishes domestic dogs from the wild painted dog (*Lycaon pictus*), "the ultimate desert hunter." *Primary iconographic scholarship.*
- Hendrickx, S., "Hunting and social complexity in Predynastic Egypt," *Bulletin des Séances / Mededelingen der Zittingen* 57 (2011, publ. 2013): 237–263. **Key methodological point:** although hunting scenes are iconographically prominent, wild-animal remains average only ~1% of faunal assemblages after ~3800 BC — hunting was symbolic/elite, not economic. This directly cautions against reading dog iconography as a literal census of working dog types.
- **Hierakonpolis Tomb 100 ("Painted Tomb"), Naqada IIC (~3500–3200 BC):** the earliest known Egyptian narrative wall painting, with hunting scenes including dogs. Case, H. & Payne, J. C., "Tomb 100: The Decorated Tomb at Hierakonpolis," *Journal of Egyptian Archaeology* 48 (1962): 5–18; Huyge, D., "The Painted Tomb, rock art and the recycling of Predynastic Egyptian imagery," *Archéo-Nil* 24 (2014): 93–102 (argues for a Naqada III modernization of a Naqada IIC original — a caution against literal reading). *Primary/scholarly.*

**The "tesem."** The term ṯzm (tesem) is the ancient Egyptian word for "hunting dog," applied in scholarship to the prick-eared, curl-tailed, leggy dog of early Egypt and also to the later lop-eared Saluki/Sloughi type. **Caution:** most detailed "tesem = ancestor of the Pharaoh Hound/Basenji/Galgo" claims circulate on enthusiast/tertiary sites (Fandom, dogbreedinfo, Egypt-tour blogs) and Wikipedia; these are **traditional claims, largely unverified in peer-reviewed literature.** The named individual dogs — **Abuwtiyuw** (6th Dynasty, elaborate royal burial documented by George Reisner from an inscribed stone found in 1935) and the "Khufu dog" Akbaru — are genuine cases of high-status dog burial, but the leap to modern breed ancestry is unsupported. Scholarly treatments of Egyptian dog burial: **Ikram, S., "Man's best friend for eternity: dog and human burials in ancient Egypt," *Anthropozoologica* 48(2):299–307 (2013);** and Osborn, D. J. & Osbornová, J., *The Mammals of Ancient Egypt* (Aris & Phillips, 1998; 2024 reissue, JSTOR).

**Saharan rock art.** The Tassili n'Ajjer (Algeria) and Acacus (Libya) rock paintings are frequently cited (e.g., Padrone 2025) as showing "graceful proto-sighthounds" from ~7000 BCE. **Caution:** dating Saharan rock art is notoriously insecure and the "sighthound" identification is interpretive — treat as suggestive, not secure. The rigorously dated Egyptian Western Desert material — Hendrickx, S., Riemer, H., Förster, F. & Darnell, J. C., "Late Predynastic/Early Dynastic rock art scenes of Barbary sheep hunting in Egypt's Western Desert" (in *Desert Animals in the Eastern Sahara*, Colloquium Africanum 4, 2009): 189–244 — shows dogs hunting Barbary sheep (~3000 BC) but does not establish a specific sighthound "breed."

**Near Eastern zooarchaeology.** Dogs are abundant at Bronze/Iron Age Near Eastern sites: the UNC dissertation "Canine Economies of the Ancient Near East and Eastern Mediterranean" documents 169 Canis specimens in a Bronze Age level (with cut marks and juveniles) and human-associated burials; at the Iron Age site of **Alaybeyi Höyük (Eastern Anatolia), over 300 dog bones were recovered from Iron Age buildings**, with 143 specimens analyzed from nine individuals (eight adults, one juvenile), notably larger than comparanda — illustrating regional size variation, not sighthound identity.

**Saluki/sighthound genetics.**
- **Larson, G. et al., "Rethinking dog domestication by integrating genetics, archaeology, and biogeography," *PNAS* 109:8878–8883 (2012), DOI 10.1073/pnas.1203005109** — identifies "basal" breeds (including Saluki, Afghan Hound, Basenji) that sit closer to the wolf root, while cautioning they are not necessarily unchanged since antiquity. *Primary.*
- **Zhang, S.-J. et al. (Zhang Ya-Ping lab), "Multiple Origins and Genomic Basis of Complex Traits in Sighthounds," *Molecular Biology and Evolution* 40(8):msad158 (2023), DOI 10.1093/molbev/msad158.** Whole-genome sequencing of 123 sighthounds: sighthounds have **multiple independent origins** in Africa, Europe, and the Middle East; morphological homogeneity results from **convergent selection** on shared pathways (bone mineral density, muscle regeneration, cardiovascular efficiency). The authors state: "The widespread need for hunting in early human societies may explain the multiple origins of sighthounds, with similar selection pressure leading to convergent evolution." This is the single most important source for the polyphyly/convergence conclusion. *Primary genetic study.*

### 4. Bronze and Iron Age Mediterranean/European Evidence

- **Western Europe synthesis:** **Horard-Herbin, M.-P., Tresset, A. & Vigne, J.-D., "Domestication and uses of the dog in western Europe from the Paleolithic to the Iron Age," *Animal Frontiers* 4(3):23–31 (2014), DOI 10.2527/af.2014-0018.** By the Iron Age (Celtic contexts), morphological selection had begun — "the shortening of the face of certain skulls and the associated pathologies, and the diversification of wither height." Ancient authors cite "some 30 'races'" of dogs characterized by geographic origin and function (pet, hunting, war/guard), including Gaulish hunting dogs such as the *Vertagus* and the Segusian hound. A dwarf dog (~27 cm withers) from the Oppidum of Rheinau, Switzerland (La Tène) is interpreted as a Mediterranean pet import (Schibler et al. 1999). The paper also documents that Late Glacial dog size already ranged widely — from "medium-sized Natoufian dogs in the Near East and their Northern Zagros contemporaries (height: 45 to 60 cm)… to very small dogs (height 30 to 45 cm or <30 cm) in Germany, Switzerland, the east of France, and the southwest and north of Spain." This is the best scholarly bridge to the emergence of functional dog types — but it postdates and is separate from any Galgo claim. *Primary/synthesis.*
- **The *Vertagus*:** classical texts (Grattius; later Arrian, in the Roman period) name a fast Gaulish coursing dog; iconographic/textual evidence for coursing dogs in Iron Age Europe exists but is generic, not breed-specific — and Arrian falls at/after the Roman threshold the brief excludes.
- **Italian Palaeolithic dogs (context):** Grotta Paglicci and Grotta Romanelli (southern Italy) yielded dogs "at least 14,000 calibrated years before present" (combined molecular/morphological study) — among the earliest Mediterranean dogs; relevant background, not sighthound-specific.

### 5. Methodological Limits (cite explicitly)

- **"Breed" is anachronistic.** Larson et al. (2012, PNAS): "Modern breeding practices, focused on distinct breeds with strict aesthetic requirements and closed bloodlines, only emerged in the 19th century, and claims for the antiquity (and long-term continuity) of modern breeds are based upon little or no historical or empirical evidence." They note broad *functional classes* (sighthounds, scenthounds, shepherd dogs) "likely [have] prehistoric roots," citing three size-classes at the ~8,000-year-old Svaerdborg site (Denmark). Use "type," not "breed," for anything pre-19th century.
- **Recreated breeds:** The Pharaoh Hound and Ibizan Hound, once thought ancient, are shown by genetics to be **recent recreations** resembling ancient sighthounds, not continuous descendants (secondary summaries of the Parker/Larson microsatellite and SNP work; consistent with Larson et al. 2012).
- **Convergent evolution:** Zhang et al. (2023) — the slender sighthound body plan arose independently multiple times via selection on the same biological pathways; **morphological similarity ≠ shared ancestry.** Downstream syntheses attribute the athletic phenotype partly to genes such as ESR1 and JAK2.
- **Osteometric limits:** Distinguishing dog from wolf skeletally relies heavily on size (the early domestic dog was smaller); morphological differences are slight (UBU/CENIEH statement; Evin et al. 2025 found no Pleistocene specimen cranially distinguishable as a dog). Inferring *function* (e.g., "cursorial sighthound") from fragmentary remains is even less secure — Daza Perea (2017) and the Iberian geometric-morphometric papers deliberately avoid functional-type labels.
- **Iconography is conventionalized, not photographic.** Hendrickx (2011): hunting imagery is symbolic (order-over-chaos, elite status), decoupled from the ~1% wild-fauna reality — so artistic "greyhound" silhouettes cannot be read as literal breed documentation. Huyge (2014) shows Tomb 100 imagery was recycled/updated, further undermining literal readings.
- **A cautionary example of over-reach:** Padrone, G., "The Origins of Sighthounds: A Multiple and Complex Genomic Framework" (2025, "Origins Series," self-archived on Academia.edu) usefully synthesizes the Zhang 2023 polyphyly finding and convergent-selection interpretation (ESR1, JAK2) and correctly states "The Galgo Español's ancestry reflects complex admixture rather than isolated origins linked to Celtic traditions." **However, this is a non-peer-reviewed independent paper; use it only as a lead, relying on Zhang et al. 2023 and Larson et al. 2012 for the underlying genetics.**

## Recommendations (for the later article-writing stage)

1. **Frame the domestication section around the 2026 *Nature* pair as the current frontier**, but foreground the explicitly unresolved status ("cannot resolve where… and how many times"). Present the 2016 dual-origin, 2020 single-origin, and 2022 dual-wolf-ancestry papers as an evolving debate, not a settled sequence. *Benchmark that would change the picture:* any Pleistocene specimen yielding a genuine *dog* (not wolf) nuclear genome older than ~16,000 years, or genome-wide data from an Iberian Palaeolithic/Mesolithic dog (e.g., re-sequencing Erralla or Muge).
2. **For Iberia, lead with the Erralla (~17,000 BP), Muge/Sado Mesolithic, and Neolithic funerary-dog evidence as the solid core**, and state the negative finding plainly: no peer-reviewed study identifies a prehistoric Iberian sighthound; sizes are medium/small (40–50 cm; some as small as <30–45 cm). Flag Pires et al. (2019) "local Iberian wolf domestication" as a strong but not universally accepted claim, now in tension with Marsh et al. (2026).
3. **For sighthound origins, make Zhang et al. (2023) the anchor**: polyphyletic, convergent. Treat all Egyptian "tesem → modern breed" and Saharan rock-art claims as *iconographically suggestive but genealogically unverified*.
4. **Keep the Galgo Español out of the deep-prehistory chain entirely in this batch**, per the brief — establish only what is known about dog/sighthound prehistory generally and in Iberia, and explicitly reserve connection/non-connection assessment for a later batch. Do not assert any unbroken lineage.
5. **Adopt a consistent citation convention distinguishing primary sources (genetic studies, archaeological reports, ancient art) from secondary/tertiary**, and tag every enthusiast-site claim "traditional claim, unverified."

## Caveats

- **Recency/instability of the 2026 findings:** The two *Nature* papers are only months old (March 2026); their specific ancestry models (Mesolithic eastern-ancestry influx; single eastern-progenitor fit for pre-Neolithic European dogs) may be revised. Report them as current best evidence, not final.
- **Conflicts in the literature:** Pires et al. (2019) argue for local Iberian wolf domestication and a non-eastern origin of haplogroup A, which does not sit comfortably with the eastern-ancestry-influx narrative of Marsh et al. (2026); present this tension rather than smoothing it over.
- **Older "proto-dog" claims (Razboinichya, Goyet, Předmostí) are morphology-based and genetically contested** — cite them as claims, with the counter-evidence (Evin et al. 2025; Bergström et al. 2026 showing Goyet is wolf-like).
- **Iberian sizes and morphotypes** come largely from mandibles/fragmentary remains; withers-height estimates via osteometric indices (Harcourt 1974, Koudelka 1885, Clark 1995) carry real error bars.
- **Some URLs are to abstract/paywalled pages** (ScienceDirect, *Science*, *Nature*); DOIs are provided for all key papers so each claim can be retrieved independently.
- **Egyptian iconographic scholarship (Hendrickx) is authoritative but interpretive**; the "tesem" typology itself is debated among Egyptologists (one greyhound-like type vs. two — the early curl-tailed prick-eared form and the later lop-eared Saluki/Sloughi form).
- **The Roman boundary:** the *Vertagus*/Segusian material and Arrian's coursing-dog descriptions sit at or just after the pre-Roman limit set by the brief; note them as context but reserve full treatment for a Roman-period batch.
