# Galgo Español, 1700–1900: Continuity of a Working Sighthound Through Two Centuries of Change

## TL;DR
- Across the 18th and 19th centuries the galgo underwent **remarkably little morphological or functional change** — it remained a swift, open-field hare-coursing dog used across all social classes — and, contrary to popular breed lore, the period's leading naturalists and dog writers (Buffon, Youatt, "Stonehenge") did **not** single out a "Spanish greyhound" as the fountainhead of the English breed; that origin story is largely a 20th-century retrospective construction.
- The genuinely documented, decisive events of the period are institutional and legal rather than zoological: the founding of Spain's first veterinary school (Madrid, opened 18 October 1793), the Hunting Law of 10 January 1879 that first regulated coursing with galgos by license and season, the first Spanish dog show (Madrid, Retiro, 1890), and the formalization of a breed standard only just after the period, with the Sociedad Central de Fomento de las Razas Caninas en España (founded 27 June 1911).
- The most consequential change to the breed itself — large-scale crossbreeding with the English Greyhound to make faster track/coursing dogs — began at the very end of the 19th century and peaked in the early 20th, and is regarded in Spanish cinófilo literature as the greatest genetic "assault" on the native race.

## Key Findings

**1. Royal hunting under the Bourbons changed in meaning, not in the dog.** The Bourbon succession (1700) brought a French-style, Enlightenment court culture. Hunting shifted from an aristocratic rehearsal for war (the Habsburg/Velázquez conception) to an aristocratic *pastime* — visible in Goya's portraits, where Charles III and Charles IV are shown resting the gun on the ground rather than posed for martial readiness. The galgo itself was a rural coursing dog, largely outside this courtly hunting-portrait tradition, which favored gun dogs and pointers.

**2. Enlightenment natural history did not enshrine the galgo as a distinct "breed."** Buffon's *Histoire naturelle* treated all dogs as one degenerated species varying by climate — famously noting the dog is "paré d'une belle robe soyeuse en Espagne" — but the concept was of climate-driven *variety*, not fixed breed. The modern taxonomic idea of "breed" with a written standard is a late-19th-century, Anglo-institutional invention that Spain adopted around 1911.

**3. Literary presence is deep but stable.** The galgo is a fixture of Spanish letters from the opening line of *Don Quixote* ("...rocín flaco y galgo corredor") onward, and it recurs in proverbs. A telling early-19th-century political proverb — *"A los galgos del Rey no se les escapa la liebre"* — satirized the corrupt government of Fernando VII. The dog functions as a symbol of hidalgo/rural identity rather than as a subject of technical description.

**4. The galgo was emphatically a cross-class, rural dog.** Unlike England and France, where sighthound coursing was legally reserved to nobility, in Spain the galgo was kept by peasants, bourgeois, and aristocrats alike; hare coursing was both subsistence and prestige activity.

**5. Institutional formalization is a late, largely post-1900 story.** First Spanish dog show: 1890, Retiro, Madrid. First national kennel body: 1911. Breed standard officially fixed: 1911.

## Details

### Royal hunting and the Bourbons
The first Bourbon, Philip V (reigned 1700–1746, the longest reign in Spanish monarchical history), imported a Versailles-derived court culture. The Bourbons continued the royal art-patronage tradition that ran from Titian and Velázquez under the Habsburgs to Goya under Charles IV.

The clearest documentary window on 18th-century royal dogs is Goya. His *Charles IV in his Hunting Clothes* (1799, Royal Palace of Madrid, 210×130 cm) and his *Charles III in Hunting Dress* (Prado; versions also in the Fernán Núñez and BBVA collections) deliberately echo Velázquez's hunting portraits of Philip IV, which Goya had etched in 1778. Art historian Julián Gallego, cited in the Colección BBVA catalogue entry for the Charles III portrait, pinpoints the key change: "At the time of King Philip IV and Velázquez, hunting was primarily viewed as a preparatory exercise for warfare, while in the age of the enlightenment and Charles III it was seen more of a pastime, and that is why probably he is depicted resting his rifle on the ground." The dogs in these portraits are gun-dog/pointer types with collars reading "REY N.o S.r" and "I belong to His Majesty," not galgos. Goya also produced hunting tapestry cartoons (e.g., *The Boar Hunt*, 1775; *Dogs on a Leash*), documenting the hunting material culture of the Bourbon court. Charles IV, per the standard art-historical assessment, "was outmanoeuvred by Napoleon at a time when he was more interested in sport and hunting than affairs of state" — a monarch temperamentally suited to the country-gentleman role Goya subtly renders.

### Natural history and the concept of "breed"
Buffon (1707–1788) published the *Histoire naturelle* in 36 volumes (1749–1788), the dog volume appearing in 1755 with his celebrated fold-out "Table de l'ordre des chiens" mapping roughly thirty dog races descending from a single ancestral stock (the shepherd dog). His theory of *dégénération* held that climate, food, and domestication drive variation within one species — an explicitly non-fixist, non-"breed" framework. Buffon specifically mentions Spain in the climate argument: the dog is "nu dans les pays les plus chauds... paré d'une belle robe soyeuse en Espagne, en Syrie." Whether Buffon's table names a discrete "lévrier d'Espagne" could not be confirmed from primary text; the greyhound-type entries reliably associated with his scheme are the lévrier, the Italian, Irish, Neapolitan, and Turkish greyhounds. A commonly circulated line — "les petits levriers... viennent d'Angleterre, d'Espagne et de Portugal" — derives from older French dictionaries (Furetière lineage), not Buffon, and should not be attributed to him.

Spain's own scientific-veterinary infrastructure arrived late but decisively: the Real Escuela de Veterinaria de Madrid opened 18 October 1793 (decreed by Charles III via Real Orden of 9 September 1788), modeled on Alfort (France, 1765), directed by Segismundo Malats and Hipólito Estévez (both Alfort-trained), and opening with 14 students. Malats published anatomy, materia medica, and pathology volumes (1793–1800), but these were overwhelmingly equine-focused — the horse was "the principal patient of the profession," and the word *veterinaria* itself descends from *albéitar* (Arabic for horse-healer). There is no evidence the early Madrid school produced a dedicated scientific-anatomical description of the galgo; the veterinary curriculum's dog content was minimal. (A 19th-century five-legged dog skeleton survives in the Complutense veterinary museum's "Gabinete de Monstruosidades," a reminder of the era's teratological rather than breed-standard interests.)

### The English-greyhound origin claim, tested
The popular modern narrative — that the galgo was "exported in the 16th–18th centuries to England and Ireland" and became "one of the parents of the current Greyhound" — is repeated by the RSCE and countless breed sites but is **poorly corroborated by primary sources.** A targeted review of the two most authoritative Victorian British dog writers found the opposite emphasis:

- **William Youatt, *The Dog* (1845):** In his origins chapter and his roster of greyhound "varieties" (rough Irish/Highland, smooth southern British, Greek, Italian, hairless African/Brazilian), Youatt names **no "Spanish greyhound"** and traces the "celeres" (dogs of speed) to the **Celtic nations**, not Spain: "the 'celeres'... [were assigned] to the Celtic nations."
- **"Stonehenge" (John Henry Walsh), *The Greyhound* (1853):** As reported in secondary literature, Walsh argued for a **Celtic origin** of the greyhound — again cutting against a Spanish-origin story. No verified verbatim passage naming Spanish greyhound blood was located.
- **George R. Jesse, *Researches into the History of the British Dog* (2 vols., 1866):** No Spanish-greyhound reference could be verified.

No named breeder, kennel, or date could be found for any 18th–19th-century importation of galgos to improve British coursing stock. The verifiable, better-documented gene flow runs the *other* way and *later*: English Greyhounds imported into Spain from the late 19th century to cross with galgos for track/coursing speed. This is a significant correction to the standard breed history. (Verifiable British coursing-institution dates that do *not* involve Spanish imports: first public coursing club, Swaffham, 1776; Waterloo Cup, 1836; National Coursing Club, 1858; Greyhound Stud Book, 1882.)

### Literature, costumbrismo, and symbolism
The galgo's literary anchor is Cervantes, and its Golden Age hunting-treatise anchor is Alonso Martínez de Espinar's *Arte de ballestería y montería* (1644; reprinted 1739 Naples, 1761 Madrid), whose vivid description of coursing hares with "galgos... ligerísimos" remained the touchstone text through the 18th century. In the Romantic and costumbrista 19th century, the great chroniclers of everyday Spanish life — Larra and Mesonero Romanos (Madrid), Estébanez Calderón and Fernán Caballero (Andalusia), Pereda (northern Castile), Pedro Antonio de Alarcón — worked precisely in the register (rural types, regional customs, local animals) where the galgo belonged, and costumbrismo explicitly documented "even local animals." The galgo's role in the culture is chiefly emblematic: the lean coursing hound as shorthand for the impoverished-but-proud hidalgo and for Castilian/Manchegan rural life. The early-19th-century proverb "A los galgos del Rey no se les escapa la liebre" shows the dog functioning as political metaphor under Fernando VII.

### Economic, social, and legal change
The pivotal legal document of the period is the **Hunting Law of 10 January 1879** (Restoration era, under Alfonso XII), the first modern Spanish hunting statute. It regulated coursing with galgos specifically: Article 34 prohibited hunting with galgos on cultivated land from 1 March to 15 October (sowing to harvest, and in vineyards from bud to vintage); Article 35 required a special governor's license costing 25 pesetas, valid one year for six persons and ten dogs. This is direct evidence that galgo coursing was widespread enough — and enough of a threat to crops — to require nationwide regulation, and that it was practiced in organized parties ("cuadrillas"). The law's class dimension is notable: it restricted galgo coursing on farmland while the older tradition had permitted it broadly across classes.

Coursing evolved from subsistence/prestige hunting toward organized sport under British influence: the English "hare coursing" model (Swaffham 1776; Waterloo Cup 1836; National Coursing Club 1858) was consciously imitated in Spain. Spain's own field-coursing competitions ("Copa Algete," "Copa de la Ina") date to the very early 20th century, and formal governing bodies followed (Club Deportivo Español 1929–32; Federación Española de Galgos 1939).

**Colonial and export dimension.** The clearest transatlantic galgo story is Argentine and post-period: the European hare (*Lepus europaeus*) was introduced to Argentina in 1888, when four pairs (per Argentina's Parques Nacionales, stock reportedly from Germany, released near Cañada de Gómez) were freed on a Santa Fe estancia for sport. This spawned a coursing culture using English greyhounds and, "to a lesser extent, descendants of the Spanish greyhound," and later local crosses (galgo criollo, galgo Barbucho Patagónico). For Spain's remaining colonies at the period's end — Cuba, Puerto Rico, the Philippines, lost in 1898 — no specific galgo documentation surfaced; their economies were plantation-export (sugar, tobacco) rather than open-field coursing landscapes.

### Artistic documentation
Beyond Goya's royal hunting portraits and tapestry cartoons, the 19th-century Spanish costumbrista and *casacón* painters (Mariano Fortuny, the Jiménez Aranda brothers, Raimundo de Madrazo, José García Ramos) and later Sorolla worked in genres that captured Spanish rural and regional life where sighthounds appear. Nineteenth-century European natural-history lithography (e.g., Schinz's *Naturgeschichte der Säugetiere*, c. 1840) illustrated generic "Windhund/greyhound" types. Illustrated hunting periodicals appeared in Spain in the 1880s–90s — *La Caza Ilustrada* (ed. Juan María de Conde) and *El Campo* (ed. Julián Settier) — providing period documentation of dogs and shows.

### The transition to formal breeding
Spain's first dog show was held over five days in the spring of 1890 (an RSCE-sourced account dates the inauguration to 4 June 1890, by the Regent Queen María Cristina) by the artificial pond in Madrid's Retiro park, organized by José de Figueroa y Torres Sotomayor, Vizconde de Irueste. 150 dogs competed; admission cost 1 peseta; the event ran a small deficit (5,752 pesetas expenses vs. 4,560 in gate plus 620 in entries = a 572-peseta shortfall). Prizes were awarded under royal patronage: a pachón named "The" won Best in Show and the Queen's prize, and a mastiff named "León" belonged to Prime Minister Antonio Cánovas del Castillo. Irueste also attempted, unsuccessfully, to found a Spanish kennel club — an idea realized in 1911 when the Sociedad Central de Fomento de las Razas Caninas en España was founded (27 June 1911; legally constituted 12 July 1911), gained royal patronage from Alfonso XIII (1 December 1911, becoming the "Real" Sociedad), joined the FCI in 1912, and recognized the galgo among its first standardized breeds, fixing the standard in 1911 in part to combat the anglo-Spanish mongrelization then underway.

## Recommendations
- **Prioritize primary-source archival work on the English-greyhound origin claim.** The standard breed narrative is currently unsupported. Direct page-checks of Youatt's greyhound chapter (Ch. II), Walsh/"Stonehenge" *The Greyhound* (1853 origin chapter), and Jesse's *Researches* (1866) greyhound sections would either confirm or (more likely) refute the claim. Treat the "galgo → English greyhound" export story as **unverified** in any publication until then.
- **Verify Buffon's dog table directly.** Inspect the 1755 "Table de l'ordre des chiens" (digitized, NYPL) and Smellie's/Barr's English translations to settle whether Buffon named a Spanish greyhound. Until then, cite Buffon only for the general climate/degeneration framework and the "belle robe soyeuse en Espagne" line.
- **Mine Spanish hunting periodicals (1880s–1900s)** — *La Caza Ilustrada*, *El Campo* — and the 1890 exhibition records for the earliest technical descriptions of "raza galgo" as a standardized type; this is the likeliest place a proto-standard predating 1911 would appear.
- **Search the Cortes debates and provincial records around the 1879 Hunting Law** for the economic/agrarian arguments about galgo coursing and crop damage — a documented, under-studied social-history vein (the Comillas University TFG/thesis on the 1879 law is a starting point).
- **Consult Dolores Carmen Morales Muñiz's "zoohistoria" corpus** for methodology and a medieval baseline; note her work concentrates on the medieval period, so the 1700–1900 window remains comparatively under-served scholarly territory.

**Benchmarks that would change these conclusions:** a dated, named 18th–19th-century record of galgo importation to Britain/Ireland (would upgrade the export claim from "unverified" to "supported"); a verified Buffon entry for a Spanish greyhound (would push the "breed vs. type" distinction earlier); a pre-1890 Spanish breed-club or written galgo standard (would move Spanish formalization out of the Anglo-imitative late-19th-century frame).

## Caveats
- **Breed-website sourcing is weak and self-repeating.** Much online galgo history (origins, "export to England," Celtic/Phoenician arrival dates) is uncited and recycled; these claims are flagged rather than asserted here.
- **The negative finding on Victorian British writers is based on partial verification** — Youatt's Chapter I was fully checked, but his dedicated greyhound sub-chapter and the full texts of Stonehenge and Jesse were not accessible page-by-page. The conclusion is "no evidence found," not "proven absent."
- **The date of the first Spanish dog show carries a minor discrepancy** (a fetched article says "May 1890"; an RSCE-sourced summary specifies 4 June 1890). Both agree on the year, venue (Retiro), organizer (Vizconde de Irueste), and 150 entrants.
- **The 1700–1900 window is genuinely thin in the historiography.** The richest scholarship clusters in the medieval/early-modern (fueros, montería treatises) and the 20th century (crossbreeding, welfare); the two centuries in scope are documented mainly through law, art, and literature rather than dedicated cynological study.
- **Continuity itself is a finding, not an absence of one:** the repeated scholarly observation that the galgo "did not apparently experience any significant change in the 18th and 19th centuries" is consistent across sources and is the period's central fact.
