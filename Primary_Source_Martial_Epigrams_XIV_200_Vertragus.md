# Martial, Epigrams XIV.200: Canis vertragus

## Direct evidence

Martial's *Epigrams* XIV.200 is a two-line epigram headed **Canis vertragus**. The Latin text is independently exposed by both the Packard Humanities Institute and The Latin Library.

The operative text reads:

> Non sibi, sed domino venatur vertragus acer,
> Inlaesum leporem qui tibi dente feret.

A conservative translation is: **the keen vertragus hunts not for itself but for its master, and will carry the hare to you unharmed in its teeth.**

## What the source directly supports

- the Latin term **vertragus** is explicitly attested in Martial XIV.200;
- the animal is a hunting dog associated with the hare;
- the epigram describes the dog as hunting for its master;
- the dog is represented as able to bring the hare back without damaging it;
- this is strong evidence for the terminology and hunting function of an ancient dog type.

## What the source does not directly support

Martial XIV.200 does **not** state that:

- the vertragus is Spanish or Iberian;
- the dog is a Galgo Español;
- the vertragus is the direct biological ancestor of the modern Galgo Español;
- there was an uninterrupted breeding population between Martial's period and modern Spain;
- the term `vertragus` is etymologically identical to `galgo`.

Martial was born at Bilbilis in Hispania, but the author's origin does not relocate every animal named in his Roman poetry to Hispania. That biographical fact must not be used as population-continuity evidence.

## Why this record matters

Popular Galgo histories often use Martial as one link in a direct-ancestry chain. The primary text supports a much narrower and more defensible proposition: **an ancient Latin source explicitly names a hare-hunting vertragus and gives it a distinctive retrieval/coursing-related function.**

That is meaningful evidence for continuity-of-function research. It is not population genealogy.
