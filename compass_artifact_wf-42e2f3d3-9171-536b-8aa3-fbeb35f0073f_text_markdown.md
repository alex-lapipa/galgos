# Batch 08 (Enriched): Early Modern Iberian History and the Galgo (1450–1700)

## TL;DR
- The documentary record from 1450–1700 shows remarkable **terminological stability**: *galgo/lebrel* (sighthound, hunts by sight), *sabueso* (scent hound), and *mastín/alano* (gripping/guard dogs) are consistently distinguished across Vallés (1556), Covarrubias (1611), and the conquest chronicles — the strongest recent statement being John Ensminger's 2022 argument that chroniclers were NOT careless with canine terminology.
- The single most important scholarly re-examination is **John J. Ensminger, "From hunters to hell hounds" (*Colonial Latin American Review* 31.3, 2022, pp. 354–380)**, which argues the first European dogs Columbus brought (1493) and used at the Battle of Vega Real (1495) were *lebreles* (sighthounds/greyhounds), not *mastines* — directly correcting Varner & Varner's *Dogs of the Conquest* (1983).
- The definitive treatise edition is **Fradejas Rueda's 1994 critical edition of Juan Vallés's *Libro de acetrería y montería*** (Círculo de Bibliofilia Venatoria, 2 vols.); Argote de Molina's *Discurso sobre el Libro de la Montería* (Seville: Andrea Pescioni, 1582) is the first Spanish hunting book and the first to depict hunting in America.

## Key Findings

### 1. Hunting treatises (1450–1700)
- **Juan Vallés, *Libro de acetrería y montería*** — completed 1 August 1556 by Juan Vallés (c. 1496–1563), treasurer general of the Cámara de Comptos Reales of Navarre (1524–1564). Six books; the sixth is devoted to dogs (*los perros*). The definitive scholarly edition is **José Manuel Fradejas Rueda (ed.), Madrid: Círculo de Bibliofilia Venatoria, 1994, 2 vols.** (ISBN 84-604-8669-9), a critical edition. Fradejas Rueda's introduction dates the second redaction before 1546 (the *Libro de la montería* section was added at the request of the Marqués de Mondéjar, viceroy of Navarre until 24 April 1546). Vallés drew explicitly on both the *Livro da montaria* of João I of Portugal ("fue de un rey de Portugal") and Gaston Fébus's *Livre de chasse* ("uno escrito en lengua francesa"), stating: "Del libro francés tomé la montería del ciervo a fuerça... y tomé tanbién d'él la montería del puerco y ciervo con redes." The task's manuscript locations (BNE mss. 3127, 3335, 3379, 3382; RAE; Biblioteca Colombina, Seville) should be verified against Fradejas Rueda's stemma; the free interim digital text is hosted at aic.uva.es (Archivo Iberoamericano de Cetrería, which Fradejas Rueda founded).
- **Argote de Molina, *Discurso sobre el Libro de la Montería*** — appended to his edition of Alfonso XI's *Libro de la montería que mandó escrevir el muy alto y poderoso rey don Alonso de Castilla y de León* (**Seville: Andrea Pescioni, 1582**), dedicated to Philip II. It is bibliographically "the first Spanish book on hunting and the first book dealing with hunting in America." It is decorated with **35 woodcut illustrations comprising eighteen different images (some recurring), three of which represent hunting scenes in the Americas** (per Santonocito 2016, pp. 240–241; the images are attributed to Juan de Arfe y Villafañe or Mateo Pérez de Alesio, and the corrida woodcut at Discurso f. 14v is considered the first printed representation of bullfighting). Pescioni had also issued Argote's edition of the *Historia del gran Tamorlán* of Ruy González de Clavijo, with its own *Discurso*. The primary text is digitized by the Universidad de Sevilla (Fondo Antiguo) and the Universidad de Valencia (Biblioteca Histórica).
- **João I of Portugal, *Livro da Montaria*** (composed 1415–1433 by João I, Master of Avis) — the standard critical edition is **Francisco Maria Esteves Pereira (ed.), Coimbra: Imprensa da Universidade, 1918** (LXV + 465 pp.), based on **manuscript no. 4352 of the Biblioteca Nacional de Lisboa**, published by order of the Academia das Ciências de Lisboa. The full text is on the Internet Archive and at purl.pt. It is an extensive boar-hunting treatise; a modern critical edition was produced by José María Abalo Buceta ("El livro da Montaria de D. João I de Portugal (1415–1433)," Universidad de Valladolid, 2009).
- **Edward of Norwich, *The Master of Game*** (written 1406–1413) — the oldest English-language hunting book, **of which 27 manuscripts survive** (the Baillie-Grohman 1904 printed edition, with a foreword by Theodore Roosevelt, is based on Cottonian MS Vespasian B. XII, c. 1420); mostly a translation of Gaston Fébus's *Livre de chasse*. **Chapter XVI, "Of Alauntes and of their Nature,"** describes three alaunt types: the *alaunt gentil* ("made and shaped as a greyhound, even of all things save of the head, the which should be great and short"), the *alaunt de boucherie* (mastiff-like, used by butchers to control cattle), and the *alaunt vautre/veantre* ("a great and ugly cur... kept only to bait the bear and wild boar"). Alaunts were described as "of Spanish origin." Edward preserved the terms later copied into Cotgrave's dictionary.
- **Gaston Fébus, *Livre de chasse*** (1387–1389, by Gaston III of Foix-Béarn) — the source text; the celebrated illuminated copy is **BnF MS Français 616**, the subject of Klemettilä's 2015 monograph. It distinguishes *lévriers* (sighthounds) from other types.

### 2. Columbus and conquest dogs — the definitive Ensminger re-examination
**John J. Ensminger, "From hunters to hell hounds: the dogs of Columbus and transformations of the human-canine relationship in the early Spanish Caribbean," *Colonial Latin American Review* 31, no. 3 (2022): 354–380** (DOI 10.1080/10609164.2022.2104035; open access, CC BY-NC-ND; also SSRN 4264814). Key findings, with verbatim quotations:

- **Central thesis (abstract):** "Greyhounds (lebreles) were the first European dogs brought to the New World in 1493 by Columbus. The Spanish had occasionally used dogs against the Moors in the final stages of the Reconquista... but the primary expectation was that the dogs would be hunters for catching game."
- **Correction of Varner & Varner:** Ensminger directly rebuts *Dogs of the Conquest* (1983), which described the second-voyage and Vega Real dogs as mastiffs. He writes: "**the dogs at Vega Real were described as lebreles, greyhounds or sighthounds, not mastines, mastiffs, and there is little reason to think that a large number of mastiffs would have been considered essential in Hispaniola.**" He rejects the Varners' premise that "one can never be certain that their designations were entirely accurate."
- **On terminological precision:** "**The burden of proof should be on anyone asserting sloppiness in the use of canine terminology, rather than an axiom that can negate any designation.**" And: "This confusion... comes from the different uses of specific terms depending on the context, **not because the chroniclers were careless with canine terminology.** Spanish writers generally saw mastiffs and greyhounds as distinct: 'diversa especie la del mastín y la del lebrel' (Acosta 1590)."
- **The archival provisioning order (crucial nuance):** Fonseca's provisioning order for the second voyage (17 ships, provisioned by the cleric Juan Rodríguez de Fonseca, 1451–1524) lists "**alanos é mastines para guardar los géneros, é para la guarda de la gente**" (alants and mastiffs to guard herds and people) — per Fernández de Navarrete (1858–59) and the *Colección de documentos inéditos* (1864–1884). Ensminger stresses this was "**a general, not a specifically military purpose**" and notes "**there is no mention of lebreles, so presumably it was believed those already on Hispaniola were reproducing well.**"
- **The "twenty dogs" at Vega Real (March 1495):** the principal source is **Bartolomé de Las Casas**, who describes "lebreles de pressa"; Herrera y Tordesillas (1601) follows him. **Ferdinand Columbus's** surviving Italian text calls them "cani corsi" (coursing/running dogs). Ensminger accepts ~20 dogs handled by men flanking the column, but regards the accompanying "100,000 Tainos" figure as wildly exaggerated (citing Fernandes et al. 2020, which caps pre-contact Hispaniola–Puerto Rico population near 80,000).
- **The transformation thesis (abstract):** after Vega Real "the use of the dogs began to shift from hunting towards war and repression... Their military value began to decline as the Indigenous became more effective at killing them in battle."
- **Feral dogs and bounties:** "**By 1587 a bounty had to be offered to those willing to hunt and kill feral dogs**" (Thacher 1903); Las Casas "held feral dogs a just retribution to the Spanish."

Corroborating primary sources:
- **Las Casas, *Brevísima relación de la destrucción de las Indias* (1552):** "enseñaron y amaestraron **lebreles, perros bravísimos** que en viendo un indio lo hacían pedazos en un credo, y mejor arremetían a él y lo comían que si fuera un puerco. Estos perros hicieron grandes estragos y carnecerías."
- **Oviedo, *Sumario* (1526) / *Historia general y natural* (1535):** on silent indigenous dogs: "Eran todos estos perros, aquí en esta e las otras islas, **mudos**, e aunque los apaleasen ni los matasen, no sabían ladrar; algunos gañen o gimen bajo cuando les hacen mal." The small native dogs (*gozques*) were eaten. The chronicle also uses "alanos: mestizos de dogo y de mastín" and notes Spanish dogs (mastines, sabuesos, alanos) barked loudly, unlike native dogs.
- **Garcilaso de la Vega (Inca), *La Florida del Inca* (1605):** the lebrel named **Bruto** single-handedly stopped the flight of four indios in the province of Ocali; Garcilaso also recounts the celebrated war-dog Becerrillo.

### 3. Terminology evolution across dictionaries and languages
- **Nebrija** (*Diccionario latino-español*, 1492; *Vocabulario español-latino*, c. 1495) established the Latin equivalences. Etymologies: *lebrel* < Latin *leporarius* ("hare-hunter," from *lepus, leporis* = hare); *galgo* < *canis gallicus* ("dog of Gaul/the Gauls").
- **Covarrubias, *Tesoro de la lengua castellana o española*** (Madrid: Luis Sánchez, 1611) — the first monolingual dictionary of Spanish (and the first monolingual dictionary of a European vernacular). Under LIEBRE it preserves the proverb "En diciembre siete galgos a una liebre." The critical edition is by Arellano & Zafra (RAE), incorporating the manuscript supplement and Noydens's 1674 additions.
- The core semantic distinction — *lebreles/galgos* hunt by sight, *sabuesos* by scent, *mastines/alanos* grip and guard — is stable from the medieval *Libro de la montería* of Alfonso XI through Vallés (1556) and Covarrubias (1611). This stability is the strongest evidence for continuity of the galgo type across the early modern period.

### 4. Royal hunting culture (Reyes Católicos / Habsburgs)
- **Charles V:** **Titian's *Portrait of Charles V with a Dog*** (Museo del Prado), painted in **1533** in Bologna — a reinterpretation of Jakob Seisenegger's 1532 full-length portrait. Seisenegger stated in a 1535 letter to Ferdinand of Austria that the hound was "an English breed" and female — a large hound, not a galgo. Portraits with dogs were a Burgundian court tradition.
- **Velázquez hunting series** for the Torre de la Parada hunting lodge (c. 1635–1636): *El príncipe Baltasar Carlos, cazador* (Prado, inscription dates it Oct. 1635–Oct. 1636) shows a large recumbent hound (perdiguero/pointer type, long ears) and a small "**galgo canela**" (tan/cinnamon greyhound); *Felipe IV cazador* shows the king with a black-faced mastiff; *El cardenal infante don Fernando cazador* shows a podenco. Copies (National Trust; Musée Goya, Castres) preserve the full composition before the canvases were trimmed.
- **Philip II** issued the *Ordenanzas de descubrimiento, nueva población y pacificación de las Indias* (Bosque de Segovia/Valsaín, 13 July 1573; published in full at El Escorial, 3 May 1576, in 148 chapters). The specific **1553 "Lomo del Grullo" hunting ordinance could not be verified** in available sources — flagged as an open archival lead (see Caveats).

### 5. Artistic and iconographic evidence
- **San Baudelio de Berlanga** (Casillas de Berlanga, Soria): the Mozarabic hermitage church, built between the last third of the 11th century (c. 1085) and the early 12th century on the Christian–Muslim frontier. The secular lower-register frescoes (*Hare Hunt*, *Deer Hunt*, hunter and horseman with hounds chasing hares, falconer) date to **c. 1125** (Prado catalogue). They are among the earliest Iberian depictions of sighthound-type dogs coursing hares. Removed and exported to the United States on the night of 23–24 June 1926; dispersed to the Met/Cloisters, Boston MFA, Indianapolis, and Cincinnati. Six fragments returned to the Prado in 1957 as an indefinite deposit, in exchange for the Romanesque apse of San Martín de Fuentidueña.

### 6. Colonial and post-colonial galgo populations
- **Argentina's hare introduction (1888)** — the ecological foundation of Argentine galgo/hare-coursing culture. Two documented figures appear in named sources: the **Sistema de Información de Biodiversidad (SIB) of Argentina's Parques Nacionales states four pairs were released** ("en 1888 en una estancia de la provincia de Santa Fe, donde se soltaron 4 parejas"), while the scientific literature (Bonino, Cossíos & Meneghetti, *Folia Zoologica* 59:9–15, 2010, following Grigera & Rapoport 1983) states "**Thirty-six *L. europaeus* were imported from Germany in 1888 at 'Estancia La Hansa' near the town of Cañada de Gómez, Province of Santa Fe.**" The release was organized by Woeltje Tietjen, German consul in Rosario, to create a game species. **A second introduction took place in 1897 near Tandil (37°19′S, 59°08′W), Buenos Aires province, with hares originating from France** (Bonino et al. 2010, citing Carman 1976); further releases occurred at Las Isletas (San Luis) and in Patagonia. The hare now occupies almost all of Argentina and four neighboring countries.

### 7. Scholarly synthesis and DNA
- **Klemettilä 2015:** Hannele Klemettilä, *Animals and Hunters in the Late Middle Ages: Evidence from the BnF MS fr. 616 of the Livre de chasse by Gaston Fébus* (Routledge, 2015) — the key study of medieval hunting-dog types, including the alaunt.
- **Morales Muñiz (UAM):** Dolores Carmen Morales Muñiz, pioneer of Spanish "zoohistoria" (she coined the term in "Zoohistoria: reflexiones acerca de una nueva disciplina auxiliar," *ETF* Serie III, 4, 1991). Her core works include *El perro en la España Medieval* (2000, Real Sociedad Canina de España research prize) and her 2022 eHumanista article (vol. 52, pp. 52–68) tracing the galgo/lebrel to *canis gallicus*, first documented in Castilian in the medieval period. She works with the Laboratorio de Arqueozoología (LAZ-UAM), alongside Arturo Morales Muñiz.
- **2023 whole-genome study:** **Wu-Lue Li, Yan-Hu Liu, et al., "Multiple Origins and Genomic Basis of Complex Traits in Sighthounds," *Molecular Biology and Evolution* 40(8):msad158 (3 Aug 2023)** (senior authors Guo-Dong Wang, Ya-Ping Zhang, Kunming Institute of Zoology; co-authors include Peter Savolainen and Hannes Lohi). The study "performed genome sequencing for 123 sighthounds, including one breed from Africa, six breeds from Europe, two breeds from Russia, and four breeds and 12 village dogs from the Middle East... supporting the multiple origins hypothesis of sighthounds." It rejects a single origin, finding development in at least three regions (Africa, Europe, Middle East) with extensive admixture and human-driven convergent selection (key genes ESR1 and JAK2 shaping the athletic morphology). This complicates simplistic "Celtic origin" (*canis gallicus*) narratives for the Galgo Español, indicating complex admixture rather than isolated descent.

## Details
Vallés's Book VI is the richest early-modern Iberian source specifically on dogs; extracting its verbatim passages on galgo/lebrel/sabueso breeding and terminology requires the Fradejas Rueda 1994 edition (the free aic.uva.es text is a usable interim). The alaunt nomenclature (gentil / de boucherie / vautre) flows from Gaston Fébus → Edward of Norwich → Cotgrave, with the terms gradually superseded by "mastiff" (English) and "dogue" (French) — a lexical extinction paralleling the disappearance of the type itself. On the conquest, note the important interpretive tension: Ensminger's headline claim that the first dogs (1493) were *lebreles* rests on his reading of the narrative chronicles of the dogs actually *used*, whereas the sole surviving *provisioning archive* (the Fonseca order) lists only *alanos é mastines*. He reconciles this by positing that lebreles were already breeding on Hispaniola. Present this as a reasoned argument, not settled fact.

## Caveats
- **The "1493 dogs = lebreles" claim is interpretive.** The only surviving provisioning archive (Fonseca order) lists "alanos é mastines," not lebreles; Ensminger's thesis rests on narrative chronicles of dogs in use. Report it as a well-argued scholarly position, not consensus.
- **Unverified within budget:** the 1553 "Lomo del Grullo" ordinance; Felipe II's import of Irish greyhounds; the precise count of Isabella's monteros (24–29); and the exact BNE/RAE/Colombina manuscript shelfmarks for Vallés. These remain open leads, not confirmed facts.
- **Figure discrepancy** in the 1888 Argentine hare introduction: "four pairs" (SIB/Parques Nacionales) vs. "36 specimens" (Bonino et al. 2010 / Grigera & Rapoport 1983) — both retained above with attribution.
- **DNA nuance:** the 2023 genomic study rejects a single origin for sighthounds and emphasizes admixture, so claims tying the Galgo Español to a pure "Celtic/canis gallicus" lineage overstate the genetics; the etymology (*canis gallicus*) is linguistic, not a genetic pedigree.
- Popular and blog sources repeating the Oviedo and Las Casas dog passages were used only where the quotations were independently corroborated against primary editions (Biblioteca Virtual Miguel de Cervantes; the oj.gob.gt and berry.edu PDFs of the *Brevísima relación*).
