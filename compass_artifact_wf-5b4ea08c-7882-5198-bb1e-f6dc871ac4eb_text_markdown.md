# Batch 05: Al-Andalus and North African Connections (711–1492 CE)
## Research Dossier for the Galgo Español Project

## TL;DR
- Hunting with hounds was a genuine, documented prestige activity among the Andalusi Muslim elite, but the surviving Arabic textual record is overwhelmingly about falconry and the trained cheetah; dog-specific coursing literature from Al-Andalus itself is thin to nonexistent, and the one Andalusi agricultural treatise that planned a chapter on dogs (Ibn al-'Awwam) lost it — a telling documentary silence rather than a positive record.
- The "Moorish origin" claim — that the Sloughi was brought to Iberia and crossed to make or influence the Galgo Español — is a breed-club tradition, NOT a documented or genetically demonstrated fact; no peer-reviewed study has ever cleanly compared Galgo and Sloughi DNA, and in the one genomic study where the Galgo was sampled it clustered with European sighthounds while the Sloughi was discarded as an admixed outlier.
- The strongest documented Islamic-period transmission is TEXTUAL, not genetic: a ninth-century Abbasid falcon-and-dog book reached Iberia and was translated into Castilian c. 1250 (*Libro de los animales que cazan*), seeding the Christian hunting-manual tradition — this is knowledge transfer, not evidence of dog-lineage transfer across the Strait.

## Key Findings

1. **Hunting was central to Andalusi elite (*furusiyya*) culture, but the dog is documentarily overshadowed by the falcon and the cheetah.** Hunting (*sayd*) formed part of *furusiyya*, the aristocratic complex of horsemanship, veterinary science, arms, and the chase that crystallized under the Abbasids and structured elite education. Princely hunting with companion animals is well attested in Andalusi material culture — caliphal ivory caskets from the Madinat al-Zahra workshops depict hunting scenes tied to rulership — but the animal most emphasized in the sources is the trained cheetah, followed by the falcon. The hound appears as a recognized but under-described participant.

2. **Ibn al-'Awwam's *Kitab al-Filaha* (Seville, late 12th c.) planned a chapter on dogs that does not survive.** The most comprehensive agricultural treatise in medieval Arabic, produced in Almohad-era Seville, survives more or less complete in 34 chapters — a work that, per the Filāḥa Texts Project (filaha.org), "mentions 585 different plants" and covers the cultivation of "more than 50 fruit trees" (Sarton 1927–48, II, pp. 424–25). The same source states verbatim: "Ibn al-'Awwām's Kitāb al-filāḥa is set out in thirty-four chapters (a thirty-fifth, on dogs, was planned but no trace of it has survived)." This is the closest Andalusi agronomy comes to canine husbandry, and it is a documented absence.

3. **The Arabic hunting-manual genre (*kutub al-bayzara*) is real and rich, but bird-centered; dogs appear as a secondary chapter.** Foundational works: Kushajim's *Kitab al-Masayid wa'l-Matarid* ("Book of Snares and Spears," 10th c., d. 961/971), described as the oldest and most extensive and based on a possibly Umayyad lost source; al-Asadi's *Jamhara fi 'ilm al-bayzara* (13th c., 578 chapters covering hunting birds "and other animals [hunting dogs, cheetahs]," five known manuscripts); and Ibn Mangli's *Uns al-mala' bi-wahsh al-fala* (Mamluk Egypt, 1371). These establish that the genre treated hounds, but the surviving named specialist work most focused on dogs is Abbasid/eastern, not Andalusi.

4. **A ninth-century Abbasid "Falcon and Dog Book" is the pivotal transmission text.** The *Kitab al-Mutawakkili* (also *Kitab al-yawarih*) by al-Bazyar, compiled for Caliph al-Mutawakkil (r. 847–861), covered both falcons AND dogs — per the modern critical edition, "what was known about falcons and dogs, especially their treatment in illness" (German: "was über Falken und Hunde bekannt war, insbesondere über deren Behandlung bei Krankheiten – ein großes Bedürfnis, denn die Beizjagd... war geradezu ein höfisches Ritual"). Full citation: Muhammad ibn 'Abdallah al-Bazyar, *Das Falken- und Hundebuch des Kalifen al-Mutawakkil: Ein arabischer Traktat aus dem 9. Jahrhundert*, ed./trans. Anna Akasoy & Stefan Georges, De Gruyter, Berlin, 7 Dec 2005, 198 pp., ISBN 978-3-05-004199-5 (Wissenskultur und gesellschaftlicher Wandel 11; Arabic and German on facing pages). The same publisher's note confirms the transmission chain: "Im 13. Jh. wurde das Werk des al-Bazyar unter Friedrich II. ins Lateinische und wohl unter Alfons dem Weisen ins Kastilische übersetzt" — in the 13th century al-Bazyar's work was translated into Latin under Frederick II and into Castilian under Alfonso the Wise.

5. **Etymology: Spanish hunting-dog vocabulary is essentially NOT Arabic.** "Galgo" derives from Latin *canis gallicus*; "podenco" is non-Arabic (both established in prior batches). Arabic influence on Spanish is real but bounded: per the standard reference (Wikipedia "Influence of Arabic on Spanish," citing Dworkin), Arabic contributed "about one thousand Arabic roots and approximately three thousand derived words, making a total of around four thousand words or 8% of the Spanish dictionary," concentrated in agriculture, architecture, administration, and science. In the hunting domain, Arabic influence is largely confined to falconry/bird terms, not hound terms. "Saluki"/"Sloughi" (Ar. *saluqi*, "of Saluq") is Arabic, but it names the eastern/North African hounds themselves, not Spanish coursing dogs. The linguistic evidence therefore argues AGAINST a strong Arabic imprint on the Spanish sighthound tradition specifically.

6. **The Sloughi's Maghrebi development is genetically supported; its Iberian-transfer role is not.** Modern genomics supports polyphyletic sighthound origins with a distinct African group, but the Sloughi is genetically hard to place, and no work links it to the Galgo.

## Details

### Subtopic 1 — Islamic hunting culture in Al-Andalus
Hunting was an integral part of *furusiyya*, the aristocratic knowledge-complex that emerged under the Abbasids and structured elite education (Qantara-Med, "Hunting"). In Al-Andalus, princely hunting is attested materially — caliphal ivory caskets from the Madinat al-Zahra workshops depict hunting imagery associated with rulership, presented as prestige gifts by Umayyad elites (Cities of Light / islamicspain.tv). The Córdoba Calendar of 961 records detailed knowledge of falcon reproduction, and falconry was institutionalized under an official titled the *Amir al-Sayd*.

The dominant hunting companion animals in the sources are the **cheetah** and the **falcon**. Qantara-Med states plainly: "The dog was widely used as a companion in the hunt, as it was in the West. The art of falconry and hunting with hounds or other animals was reserved primarily for the court... Hunting with a companion animal was a princely pursuit and very often the animal was a cheetah." This is significant for the project: hound-coursing existed and was elite, but the **cheetah**, not the sighthound, is the emblematic Andalusi/Islamic elite hunting predator in the record, and panther/cat skins and captured wild cats for hunting parks feature more prominently in the trade and court record than hunting dogs do.

**Assessment:** Hare coursing with sighthounds as a *specific* elite Andalusi practice is plausible and consistent with the general picture, but no Andalusi primary text was found that foregrounds hound-coursing of hares as a named prestige sport in the way later Christian Castilian texts would. Label: elite hunting-with-hounds in Al-Andalus = documented in general terms (peer-reviewed/museum scholarship); hare coursing specifically as an Andalusi elite sport = reasonable inference, not directly attested.

### Subtopic 2 — Arabic hunting and falconry texts (the *bayzara* genre and dogs)
- **Kushajim, *Kitab al-Masayid wa'l-Matarid*** (10th c., d. 961/971) — per Qantara, the oldest and most extensive of the genre, treating fox hunting and falconry; based on a possibly Umayyad lost source. Al-Bazyar's book drew on it (uncredited).
- **al-Bazyar, *Kitab al-Mutawakkili*** (mid-9th c.) — the "Falcon and Dog Book" discussed in Key Finding 4. Survives via a 13th-century compilation, *al-Kitab al-Mansuri fi al-bayzara*, made for a Hafsid ruler of Tunisia; the Arabic of al-Mansuri was edited by Detlef Möller (1965). Veterinary care of hunting dogs is present in the text.
- **al-Asadi, *Jamhara fi 'ilm al-bayzara*** (13th c.) — 578 chapters covering hunting birds "and other animals (hunting dogs, cheetahs)"; five known manuscripts (e.g., Hagia Sophia MS 3814, dated 1273; Escorial Árabe 903). Base text for Ibn Mangli.
- **Ibn Mangli, *Uns al-mala' bi-wahsh al-fala*** (Mamluk Egypt, 1371–72; author d. c. 1382) — single manuscript (BnF Arabe 2832); edited/translated by Florian Pharaon as *Traité de vénerie* (Paris, 1880). Wide-ranging on hunting methods including dogs and the celebrated cheetah-taming process; criticizes al-Asadi's skepticism. Late and non-Andalusi, but genre-defining and the best illustration of what such manuals typically cover on dogs.
- **Ibn Hudayl** (Granada, Nasrid period, d. c. 1409) — the leading Andalusi *furusiyya* author, wrote *Kitab tuhfat al-anfus wa-shi'ar sukkan al-Andalus* (a jihad/cavalry treatise under Muhammad V) and *Hilyat al-fursan wa-shi'ar al-shuj'an*, edited/translated by Louis Mercier. These are combat/horsemanship works with veterinary and hunting content — the closest Andalusi analog to the genre, but focused on war and horses, not coursing hounds.

**Assessment:** No surviving dedicated Arabic treatise on hunting DOGS was composed in Al-Andalus. Dog content exists as chapters within bird/hunting compendia; the most dog-focused survivor is the Abbasid *al-Mutawakkili* book, which reached Iberia in translation.

### Subtopic 3 — The Sloughi and North African sighthounds
Breed-club and tertiary narratives (AKC, FCI clubs, Yabiladi) trace the Sloughi to Berber/Bedouin North Africa (Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia, Libya), invoke Neolithic Tassili rock art and Egyptian tomb imagery, and offer dates as early as 7000–8000 BCE. These are traditional claims, largely unverified as direct lineage evidence: rock art and tomb painting show lop-eared sighthound-type dogs, which supports the antiquity of the *type* in the region, not unbroken *breed* continuity to the modern Sloughi.

Peer-reviewed genetics: The 2023 study — Li, Liu, Li, et al. (senior authors Guo-Dong Wang and Ya-Ping Zhang), "Multiple Origins and Genomic Basis of Complex Traits in Sighthounds," *Molecular Biology and Evolution* 40(8):msad158, doi:10.1093/molbev/msad158, PMID 37433053 — sequenced 123 sighthounds "including one breed from Africa, six breeds from Europe, two breeds from Russia, and four breeds and 12 village dogs from the Middle East." It established that sighthounds are **polyphyletic**, with distinct genetic flows from Africa, Europe, and the Middle East, and that their morphological homogeneity results from human-driven convergent selection on the same performance pathways (key genes ESR1, JAK2; 17 positively selected genes in the African population, 27 in the European, 54 in the Middle Eastern). African sighthounds carried 16.93% Middle Eastern and 10.46% European ancestry. Crucially, the Sloughi could not be cleanly placed: "The Sloughi individual (marked by asterisks) was excluded from selection and population analysis due to genetic admixture and its outlier position in PCA, neighbor-joining tree, and structure analysis." A related mtDNA result (Kazakh Tazy study) notes the Sloughi shares no sequences with the Tazy while the Saluki does, consistent with the traditional Sloughi–Saluki relatedness. The first morphometric (not genetic) baseline for the Algerian Sloughi is recent (Haddam et al. 2024, *S. Afr. J. Anim. Sci.* 54).

**Assessment:** Maghrebi development of the Sloughi is well supported; deep-antiquity single-origin claims are unverified breed lore. In current genomic datasets the Sloughi behaves as an admixed, difficult-to-place breed.

### Subtopic 4 — Trade and canine mobility across the Strait of Gibraltar
The Almoravids (ruling Al-Andalus c. 1090–1147) and Almohads (1147–1269) politically unified Al-Andalus with Morocco, and merchants "moved back and forth freely, carrying Spanish goods"; under both dynasties Marrakech and Fez became "extensions of the Andalusi urban economy," with Jewish trading families owning houses on both sides of the Strait (Muslim Heritage, drawing on Thomas Glick). This created ample opportunity for the movement of animals, including elite hunting animals.

However, NO specific primary-source record was found of dogs — as opposed to cloth, copper, panther/cat skins, luxury objects, or wild cats/cheetahs for hunting parks — being traded or imported across the Strait. The movement of hunting dogs is therefore an **inference** from general trade and political integration, not a documented fact. This must be stated honestly: elite canine exchange across the Strait during the Islamic period is plausible and even likely in principle, but it is undocumented in the specific record.

### Subtopic 5 — Christian–Islamic exchange of dogs and hunting knowledge
The clearest, best-documented channel is TEXTUAL transmission of hunting science:
- The *Libro de los animales que cazan* (completed c. 1250, the first Castilian hunting treatise, attributed to the circle of Alfonso X; a translation of al-Bazyar's *Kitab al-yawarih* / *Kitab al-Mutawakkili*, associated with the reign of Fernando III) fed directly into the *Libro de la montería* (Alfonso XI) and shaped the Castilian hunting-manual tradition through the 17th century (editions and studies by José Manuel Fradejas Rueda; see also the 2025 *Journal of Medieval Iberian Studies* article on the fourteenth-century royal hunt).
- The parallel Latin "Moamin" (*De Scientia Venandi per Aves*, translated by Theodore of Antioch for Frederick II, 1240–41) likewise contained a chapter on hunting with dogs and circulated widely in ~27 Latin manuscripts.

Etymology (see Key Finding 5): Spanish absorbed roughly 4,000 words / ~8% of its dictionary from Arabic, overwhelmingly in agriculture, architecture, administration, and science. Hunting-dog terminology is not among them; Arabic hunting influence in Spanish is concentrated in falconry/bird vocabulary.

**Assessment:** Christian nobility clearly adopted Andalusi/Arabic-transmitted hunting KNOWLEDGE via translated manuals. Evidence for adoption of a specifically Andalusi hunting-DOG tradition, or of Arabic hound vocabulary, is absent.

### Subtopic 6 — Evidential assessment of the "Moorish origin" hypothesis
The popular claim (Wikipedia, rescue organizations, breed guides): the Galgo Español descends from, or was crossed with, Sloughis brought by the Moors. Even Wikipedia hedges it, noting the pre-existing Celtic *vertragus* in Roman Iberia (Arrian, Martial — covered in prior batches) makes a Central-European-type origin more likely, with Sloughi crossing merely "thought to have" occurred "centuries later."

Direct genetic test of the claim — the decisive contribution of this batch:
- **Parker et al. 2017** (Cell Reports 19(4):697–708, doi:10.1016/j.celrep.2017.03.079, PMID 28445722; 161 breeds, 1,346 dogs, >150,000 SNPs): Galgo Español **ABSENT**. Sighthounds fall in the "Mediterranean" clade (representative breed: Saluki). The paper separately flags that a Sloughi and country-of-origin Salukis "do not cluster with the rest of their breed."
- **Plassais et al. 2019** (Nature Communications 10:1489, doi:10.1038/s41467-019-09373-w, PMID 30940804; 722 WGS): Galgo **ABSENT**.
- **Li/Zhang et al. 2023** (MBE msad158): Galgo **PRESENT but only n=1**, counted among the "six European breeds" (with Greyhound, Irish Wolfhound, Scottish Deerhound, Italian Greyhound, and Magyar Agár/Hungarian Greyhound). It grouped with EUROPEAN sighthounds; because the Sloughi was excluded as an outlier, no Galgo–Sloughi relationship could be assessed.
- **Dog10K Genomes Project** (consortium paper Genome Biol. 2023;24:187, doi:10.1186/s13059-023-03023-7, PMID 37582787): "Galgo Espanol" IS in the sampled breed list (alongside Sloughi, Azawakh, Ibizan Hound, and others), but no published main-text statement places the Galgo relative to the Sloughi. This is a live data gap: the raw material for a direct comparison now exists, but the analysis has not been published.

**No peer-reviewed study has ever directly compared Galgo Español and Sloughi DNA head-to-head.** The only claim of such a comparison comes from a non-peer-reviewed breed-club source (Sloughi Club UK) reporting a Mars-marker microsatellite/SNP dataset, which actually concluded that Sloughi, Saluki, Azawakh, and Galgo are "genetically distinct from one another" — i.e., even that low-quality source contradicts the interbreeding narrative, and it acknowledged having essentially no genuine North African Sloughi samples.

This precisely parallels the **Batch 02 Podenco finding**: a romantic import narrative ("Phoenician" for the Podenco; "Moorish/Sloughi" for the Galgo) that genetics does not support. Where the Galgo has actually been genotyped, it sits with European sighthounds.

**Verdict:** The "Moorish/Sloughi origin" of the Galgo Español is a **traditional claim, unverified** — and, to the extent it can currently be tested, it is contradicted by the placement of the Galgo among European sighthounds and by the total absence of any direct Galgo–Sloughi genetic linkage. Do NOT assert a Sloughi→Galgo lineage in the project's articles.

## Recommendations
1. **Write the Al-Andalus articles around a documented-absence thesis for dogs.** Frame the period as one in which elite hunting culture was real and prestigious, but the specific hound-coursing record is thin, the cheetah and falcon dominate the sources, and the lost dog-chapter of Ibn al-'Awwam is the emblem of this silence — a continuity with the near-total documentary silence found for the Visigothic period in the prior batch, now for a different reason (bird/cheetah dominance rather than absence of secular literature).
2. **Foreground the *al-Mutawakkili* → *Libro de los animales que cazan* transmission chain** as the concrete, citable Islamic→Christian hunting-knowledge bridge — but explicitly separate knowledge transfer from dog-lineage transfer. Cite the Akasoy & Georges (2005) edition and Fradejas Rueda's Castilian editions directly.
3. **Treat the "Moorish origin" claim as a case study in breed mythology**, paired with the Batch 02 Podenco parallel, and state the genetic null result plainly rather than leaving it as an open "possible influence."
4. **Benchmarks that would change these conclusions:** (a) discovery or critical edition of an Andalusi text specifically on coursing hounds; (b) a peer-reviewed genomic study directly comparing multiple Galgo and Sloughi individuals (a Dog10K breed-level analysis is now the most likely route) showing shared African-derived ancestry in the Galgo; (c) zooarchaeological ancient-DNA from Islamic-period Iberian dog remains showing North African affinity. Absent any of these, the conservative position holds.
5. **Sourcing hierarchy for the finished articles:** prioritize Akasoy & Georges (2005), Fradejas Rueda's editions, Li/Zhang 2023 (MBE, doi:10.1093/molbev/msad158), Parker 2017 (doi:10.1016/j.celrep.2017.03.079), Plassais 2019 (doi:10.1038/s41467-019-09373-w), and the Dog10K consortium paper (doi:10.1186/s13059-023-03023-7); the Filāḥa Texts Project (filaha.org) for Ibn al-'Awwam; and Qantara-Med for the material-culture/hunting overview. Use breed-club and rescue websites ONLY to identify claims requiring debunking, never as positive evidence.

## Caveats
- Several genetics results rest on very small Galgo/Sloughi sample sizes (often n=1), so even the "Galgo = European sighthound" placement should be reported as provisional and single-individual.
- The Dog10K Galgo data exists but its clade placement is not yet published in accessible main text; this is a genuine, current gap rather than a settled finding.
- Much of the North African and breed-antiquity material online is tertiary breed-club content; only type-antiquity from rock art / tomb imagery and the peer-reviewed genetics should be treated as reliable, and even the former establishes type, not breed continuity.
- The Arabic textual record surveyed here was accessed via translations, editions, and secondary scholarship, not original manuscript collation; article authors should verify specific dog passages directly in the Akasoy/Georges (al-Mutawakkili) and Fradejas Rueda (*Libro de los animales que cazan*) editions before quoting them as primary evidence.
- The al-Bazyar attribution and the identity of "Moamin" are genuinely disputed in the scholarship (François Viré proposed Hunayn ibn Ishaq; the *Kitab al-mutawakkili* was found in the 1980s not to be identical with the Latin Moamin's Arabic source). Present the transmission chain as well-established in outline but contested in detail.
