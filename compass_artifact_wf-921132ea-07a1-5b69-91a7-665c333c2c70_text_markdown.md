# Batch 04: Late Antiquity and the Visigothic Period — Dogs, Hunting, and the Evidential Record (c. 400–711 CE)

## TL;DR
- **The Visigothic period (c. 507–711 CE) is a documentary near-silence on hunting dogs specifically**: the two richest surviving Hispano-Visigothic text corpora — Isidore of Seville's *Etymologiae* (c. 615–630s) and the *Liber Iudiciorum* (654) — discuss dogs only as generic animals (etymology, loyalty, guarding, breeding) or as dangerous/property animals, never as coursing sighthounds or a "Gallic"/"galgo"-type hunting breed.
- **The single explicit "hunting dog" reference from the wider Visigothic realm is ecclesiastical and prohibitive**: Canon 55/52 of the Council of Agde (506 CE, Visigothic Gaul under Alaric II) forbade bishops, priests, and deacons to keep "dogs for hunting" (*canes ad venandum*) or hawks — evidence that elite hunting with dogs existed and mattered enough to legislate against, but not a description of any breed.
- **No unbroken lineage to the modern Galgo Español can be honestly asserted for this period.** The Roman-era coursing evidence (Arrian's *vertragus*; Hispano-Roman coursing mosaics) and the modern breed bracket a ~200-year Visigothic gap in which sighthounds/coursing simply are not documented in the surviving sources; this absence is itself the honest finding.

## Key Findings

1. **Isidore of Seville, *Etymologiae* XII.2.25–28 (De bestiis / on dogs)** — primary text — gives an etymology of *canis* and a lyrical praise of canine loyalty and sagacity, and it does distinguish dogs by function ("boldness or speed") and by cross-breeding (with wolves = *lycisci*; with Indian tigers), **but names no sighthound, no coursing dog, and nothing resembling *canis gallicus* or "galgo."** It is a literary compilation from Pliny/Solinus, not an observation of Iberian hunting practice.

2. **The *Liber Iudiciorum* (Visigothic Code, 654, S.P. Scott trans. 1910)** treats dogs only in Book VIII, Title IV ("Concerning Injury to Animals"), Laws XIX–XX — as vicious/dangerous animals creating liability — and implicitly as property in the animal-loan and animal-injury framework. Its only *venatio* provisions (Book VIII.IV.XXII–XXIII) concern **traps and snares for wild beasts, not dogs**. There is no legal category of "hunting dog," no coursing, and no hunting-rights law.

3. **Ecclesiastical evidence**: The one clear Visigothic-realm reference to hunting dogs is the hunting-dog canon of the **Council of Agde (506 CE)**. The Visigothic-era **Councils of Toledo (400–702)** contain **no** canon on clergy hunting or hunting dogs — the clerical-hunting prohibition is a Gallic, not Toledan, tradition.

4. **Hunting culture**: Elite hunting certainly existed (the Agde canon assumes clergy could acquire hunting dogs and hawks; villa/estate continuity and later Iberian falconry point the same way), but Visigothic Hispania left us **no hunting treatise, no equivalent of Arrian, and no securely published corpus of Visigothic-period hunting-dog remains** comparable to the Roman coursing mosaics of the previous batch.

5. **Continuity/gap assessment**: Modern scholarship (Roger Collins, E.A. Thompson, and the Spanish/Isidorian-studies tradition) treats the period as textually rich in theology, law, and history but **thin on secular material culture and everyday practice such as hunting** — a genuine evidential gap rather than proof of discontinuity.

## Details

### 1. Isidore of Seville, *Etymologiae*, Book XII "De animalibus"

**Author, date, work.** Isidore, bishop of Seville (c. 560–636), compiled the *Etymologiae* (also *Origines*) between c. 615 and the early 630s at the request of Braulio of Zaragoza, who divided it into 20 books. Book XII, *De animalibus*, treats four-footed animals, reptiles, fish, and birds. It is heavily derivative: an analysis by Jacques André identifies for Book XII 58 quotations from named authors and 293 uncited borrowings (79 from Solinus, 61 from Servius, 45 from Pliny the Elder); Ambrose's *Hexameron* is the most-used Church Father.

**Editions and translations (for the follow-up article).**
- Latin critical edition: W. M. Lindsay, *Isidori Hispalensis Episcopi Etymologiarum sive Originum Libri XX* (Oxford: Clarendon, 1911), still in print.
- Full Latin text online (Lindsay's text): LacusCurtius, Bill Thayer, University of Chicago — Book XII at `https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/L/Roman/Texts/Isidore/12*.html`; also The Latin Library `https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/isidore/12.shtml` and Latin Wikisource `https://la.wikisource.org/wiki/Etymologiarum_libri_XX/Liber_XII`.
- Standard English translation: Stephen A. Barney, W. J. Lewis, J. A. Beach, Oliver Berghof (with M. Hall), *The Etymologies of Isidore of Seville* (Cambridge University Press, 2006; ISBN 9780521837491). Book XII = pp. 247–270. A second complete English translation is Priscilla Throop's (2005), based on Lindsay.
- Bilingual Latin-Spanish: J. Oroz Reta & M.-A. Marcos Casquero (eds.), *Etimologías: edición bilingüe*, introd. by Manuel C. Díaz y Díaz (2nd edn, Madrid: BAC, 1993).

**What Isidore actually says about dogs — primary text.** The dog passage is *Etymologiae* XII.2.25–28 (the chapter *De bestiis*, "beasts"). The verbatim Latin (Lindsay/LacusCurtius) reads:

> "**[25]** Canis nomen Latinum Graecam etymologiam habere videtur; Graece enim κύων dicitur. Licet eum quidam a canore latratus appellatum existiment, eo quod insonat; unde et canere. Nihil autem sagacius canibus; plus enim sensus ceteris animalibus habent. **[26]** Namque soli sua nomina recognoscunt; dominos suos diligunt; dominorum tecta defendunt; pro dominis suis se morti obiciunt; voluntarie cum domino ad praedam currunt; corpus domini sui etiam mortuum non relinquunt. Quorum postremo naturae est extra homines esse non posse. In canibus duo sunt: aut fortitudo, aut velocitas. **[27]** Catuli abusive dicuntur quarumlibet bestiarum filii. Nam proprie catuli canum sunt, per diminutionem dicti. **[28]** Lycisci autem dicuntur, ut ait Plinius, canes nati ex lupis et canibus, cum inter se forte miscuntur."

In the Barney et al. translation, the sense is: the name *canis* seems to have a Greek etymology (*κύων*); some think it comes from *canor* (the sound of barking). "Nothing is more sagacious than dogs, for they have more sense than other animals." They alone recognize their own names; they love their masters; they defend their masters' homes; they will die for their masters; they willingly run to the hunt (*ad praedam*, "to the prey") with their master; they do not abandon their master's body even in death; their nature is such that they cannot exist apart from humans. **"In dogs there are two [qualities]: either boldness (*fortitudo*) or speed (*velocitas*)."** Puppies (*catuli*); *lycisci* are dogs born from wolves and dogs interbreeding (citing Pliny 8.148); Indians are said to tie female dogs in the woods to mate with tigers.

**Assessment for the Galgo question.**
- Isidore's *"aut fortitudo, aut velocitas"* is the closest he comes to a functional taxonomy of dogs, and *velocitas* (speed) is exactly the quality a coursing sighthound is bred for. But **Isidore names no breed, no *vertragus*, no *canis gallicus*, no sighthound type, and gives no Iberian or coursing detail.** The phrase *voluntarie cum domino ad praedam currunt* ("they willingly run to the prey/hunt with their master") is generic praise of hunting dogs, not a breed description.
- The famous longer "numerous breeds of dogs… some track… others guard… house-dogs" passage sometimes quoted (e.g., in T.H. White's *Book of Beasts*) is from a **12th-century Latin bestiary that expands Isidore**, not from Isidore's own text — a common conflation to flag as a *tertiary/derivative claim, not Isidorian*.
- **Etymology of "galgo":** According to the Real Academia Española's *Diccionario de la lengua española* (as reproduced by Wiktionary), *galgo* derives "from Vulgar Latin *gallicus* ('Gaulish', adjective) through ellipsis of *canis gallicus* ('Gaulish dog'), from *Gallia*." **This term does not appear in Isidore's dog chapter.** Any claim that Isidore provides the etymology of "galgo" is a *traditional claim, unverified* and, on the evidence of the text itself, **false**. (For continuity with earlier batches: the classical coursing-dog attestation is the *vertragus* in Arrian's *Cynegeticus* and in Martial's *Epigrams*, per the Galgo Español breed literature; an Ovid attestation is sometimes asserted but was not verified here.)

**Hunting (*venatio*) generally in Isidore.** Isidore treats hunting terminology incidentally (e.g., forest/woodland vocabulary in Book XVII; *cuniculus*, the rabbit, XII.1.24, "so called because it is caught by the tracking of dogs [*canum indagine*] or driven from its burrows" — a genuine hunting-with-dogs reference). He wrote no dedicated treatise on the hunt.

### 2. Visigothic Law: the *Liber Iudiciorum* and its antecedents

**The texts and their dating.**
- *Codex Euricianus* (Code of Euric), c. 475–480, the earliest written Visigothic law, from the Toulouse kingdom.
- *Lex Romana Visigothorum* / Breviary of Alaric (506), a Roman-law compendium for the kingdom's Roman subjects — the lens through which Isidore knew Roman law (Book V of the *Etymologiae*).
- *Liber Iudiciorum* (also *Lex Visigothorum*, *Forum Iudicum*; Spanish *Fuero Juzgo*): begun under Chindasuinth (642–643), promulgated in its territorial form by **Reccesuinth in 654** at the Eighth Council of Toledo; revised by Ervig (681) and Egica (687–702). Twelve books.
- Latin critical edition: **Karl Zeumer, *Leges Visigothorum*, MGH Leges nationum Germanicarum I (Hanover/Leipzig, 1902).**
- English translation: **S. P. Scott, *The Visigothic Code (Forum Judicum)* (Boston, 1910)** — the only complete English translation; online at documentacatholicaomnia.eu, libro.uca.edu/vcode/, Fordham Sourcebooks, ra.smixx.de, and Project Gutenberg ebook 72551. (Scott worked from the last, revised recension; there is still no full English translation of the *Liber* in its original Reccesuinthian form. Alexandra Wilhelmsen translated Book VI in an academic thesis.)

**What the Code says about dogs — primary text (Scott's rubrics, verbatim).** All dog and animal material sits in **Book VIII ("Concerning Acts of Violence and Injuries"), Title IV ("Concerning Injury to Animals, and Other Property")**:
- Law XVI: "Where a Vicious Animal, while on the Premises of its Owner, Kills Anyone."
- Law XVII: "Where Anyone Rids himself of a Vicious Animal, or Still Retains it in his Possession."
- Law XVIII: "Where Anyone Teases an Animal and is Injured by it, he Alone shall be Responsible for the Injury."
- **Law XIX: "Where a Dog that has been Irritated, whether the Provocation was Wanton or not, is Proved to have Injured, or Killed Anyone."**
- **Law XX: "Concerning a Vicious Dog."**
- Law XXI: "Concerning Injuries to Clothing."
- **Law XXII: "Where a Trespasser Falls into a Trap set for Wild Animals."**
- **Law XXIII: "He who sets Snares or Traps for Wild Beasts, must Inform his Neighbors of the Places where said Snares are Set."**

Animals also appear as property in **Book V, Title V** ("Concerning Property Committed to the Charge of, or Loaned to, Another"), Law I ("Concerning Animals Hired to Another") and Law II ("Concerning Animals Loaned for the Purpose of Labor"); and theft of animals/cattle-bells in Book VII, Title II (e.g., Law XI, "Concerning the Stealing of Bells from Cattle").

**Assessment.** The Visigothic Code contains **no provision on hunting dogs, sighthounds, coursing, or hunting rights.** Dogs appear only (1) as dangerous/vicious animals generating liability, and (2) implicitly as property. The only hunting-related laws concern **traps and snares for wild beasts (*venatio*), and mention no dogs at all.** This is a striking silence given that the same code legislates in detail on bees, pigs, straying cattle, and trees. In secular Visigothic law, there is simply no legal category of "hunting dog."

### 3. Ecclesiastical evidence

**The Council of Agde (Concilium Agathense), 506 CE — the key text.** Held at Agde in Visigothic Gaul with the permission of King Alaric II and presided over by Caesarius of Arles, the council convened on 10/11 September 506; per the Presbyters Project (University of Warsaw, drawing on Munier's 1963 edition) it "was attended by 24 bishops, and 8 presbyters and 2 deacons, who represented their bishops," and promulgated 47 genuine canons. The hunting-dog canon is rubricated *"Ut sacerdotes et levitae canibus ad venandum et accipitribus non utantur"* ("That priests and deacons shall not use dogs for hunting, or hawks"). The verbatim body, in Munier's critical edition (CCSL 148; MGH digitization, benedictus.mgh.de, "Concilium Agatense"), reads:

> "Episcopis presbiteris diaconibus, canes ad venandum aut accipitres habere non liceat. Quod si quis talium personarum in hac voluntate detectus fuerit, si episcopus est tribus mensibus se a communione suspendat, presbiter duobus mensibus se abstineat, diaconus uno ab omni officio vel communione [cessabit]."

("It shall not be lawful for bishops, priests, or deacons to keep dogs for hunting, or hawks. If any such person is caught in this inclination, if he is a bishop let him suspend himself from communion for three months; a priest shall abstain for two months; a deacon shall cease from all office or communion for one month.")

- **Canon numbering:** the canon is cited as **55** in the older Sirmond tradition (rubric "LV. Ut sacerdotes & levitae, canibus ad venandum & accipitribus non utantur") but as **52** ("LII") in the MGH edition of the Hispana rubric-list. The *text* is stable across editions.
- Editions: C. Munier (ed.), *Concilia Galliae A. 314 – A. 506*, CCSL 148 (Turnhout: Brepols, 1963), pp. 189–228; Mansi, *Collectio* VIII, col. 323 ff.; Hefele, *History of the Councils*, vol. II, pp. 649–660.
- This canon passed into the *Corpus Iuris Canonici* under the title *"De clerico venatore"* (Decretals of Gregory IX), becoming the foundational medieval canon against clerical hunting.

**Significance for the Galgo project.** This is the **one explicit "hunting dog" (*canes ad venandum*) reference in the Visigothic-realm legal/ecclesiastical record.** It confirms that hunting with dogs was a live elite practice (clergy were being drawn into it), but it (a) names **no breed**, (b) describes **no coursing technique**, and (c) is prohibitive, not descriptive. It cannot support any claim about a specific Iberian sighthound.

**Councils of Toledo (400–702).** The Visigothic-era Toledo councils — including Toledo III (589, Reccared's conversion from Arianism) and Toledo IV (633, presided over by Isidore) — legislated on heresy, clerical celibacy/education, church property, liturgy, royal succession, and (repeatedly and harshly) on Jews. **None contains a canon on hunting or hunting dogs.** The clerical-hunting prohibition in Visigothic territory is therefore Gallic (Agde), not Toledan. (Caveat: this rests on comprehensive scholarly summaries of the councils rather than a canon-by-canon autopsy of all eighteen; but the topic is consistently tied only to Agde in the canon-law tradition.)

**Hagiography and secular literature.** King Sisebut (r. 612–621), the most literate Visigothic monarch, wrote the *Vita vel passio Sancti Desiderii* and a poem on lunar eclipses (*De eclipsi lunae*) sent to Isidore; the anonymous *Vitas Sanctorum Patrum Emeretensium* (Lives of the Fathers of Mérida) is another major hagiographic source. These texts (ed./trans. John R. C. Martyn, *King Sisebut and the Culture of Visigothic Spain*, 2008) illuminate court culture but, on present evidence, **contain no coursing or sighthound material** — a documented negative worth stating.

### 4. Hunting culture in Visigothic Hispania

- **That elite hunting existed** is inferable from (a) the Agde canon (clergy acquiring hunting dogs and hawks), (b) the continuity of Roman *villa* and estate life into the Visigothic period, and (c) later Iberian continuity of hunting/falconry. This is reasonable inference, not direct description.
- **Material culture**: Visigothic elite identity is best represented archaeologically by metalwork — eagle-shaped fibulae (e.g., the garnet-and-glass eagle brooches from Tierra de Barros, Badajoz) and belt buckles with animal/eagle iconography adopted from Roman imperial insignia. This iconography is **symbolic/heraldic (eagles), not hunting-scene or dog imagery**; it does not document coursing.
- **Faunal/archaeological dog remains**: Iberian dog remains are well studied for the Palaeolithic through Chalcolithic (e.g., the multidisciplinary study of Chalcolithic Iberian *Canis* at Leceia, Portugal, and El Casetón de la Era, Valladolid) and for the Roman period, but **no comparably published, securely dated corpus of Visigothic-period hunting-dog remains from Iberia surfaced in this research** — a gap to state plainly and, if the follow-up article needs it, to pursue in Spanish-language zooarchaeological literature (CSIC, Dialnet).
- **Continuity vs. discontinuity with Roman coursing**: The Hispano-Roman coursing mosaics (previous batch) show organized hare-coursing with sighthounds. Nothing in the Visigothic textual record either confirms or denies the survival of that specific practice. Latin remained the written language and Roman estate structures persisted, which favors general cultural continuity; but **the specific chain "Roman coursing → Visigothic coursing → medieval/modern galgo" is undocumented for these two centuries.**

### 5. Continuity and evidential gaps — honest assessment

- **This is a period of relative silence regarding hunting dogs specifically.** The two best Hispano-Visigothic sources that *should* mention them if they were a notable category — Isidore's encyclopedia and the territorial law code — treat dogs only generically or as hazards/property. The only explicit hunting-dog reference (Agde 506) is Gaulish, prohibitive, and breed-blind.
- **Modern scholarship** on Visigothic Spain (Roger Collins, *Visigothic Spain 409–711* [Blackwell, 2004]; E. A. Thompson, *The Goths in Spain* [Oxford, 1969]; José Orlandis; and the Isidorian-studies tradition) emphasizes the richness of 7th-century Hispanic *learned* culture while repeatedly noting how **thin the evidence is for secular daily life and material practice**, and how dependent we are on "centre-ist" royal and ecclesiastical sources. Hunting culture falls squarely into the under-documented zone.
- **Bottom line for the Galgo Español narrative**: The Visigothic period should be presented as an **evidentiary gap/bridge**, not a proven link. The honest, defensible statement is: *sighthounds and coursing are documented in Roman Hispania and again in the later medieval and modern periods, but the Visigothic sources are silent on coursing dogs specifically; elite hunting with dogs certainly existed (per Agde 506), but no source of the period describes a sighthound or a "galgo"-type dog.*

## Recommendations

1. **Write the Isidore article around the actual XII.2.25–28 text**, quoting the Latin and the Barney et al. translation, and make the *"aut fortitudo, aut velocitas"* line the analytical centerpiece — while explicitly correcting two common errors: (a) the "numerous breeds… some track, others guard" passage is a **later bestiary expansion, not Isidore**; (b) Isidore does **not** give the etymology of "galgo"/*canis gallicus* (that etymology comes from the RAE dictionary and the classical *vertragus* tradition, not from Isidore). Label both as unverified/derivative.
2. **Write the law article around Book VIII, Title IV, Laws XIX–XX and XXII–XXIII**, framing the finding as a *documented silence*: dogs as hazard/property, hunting only via traps/snares, no hunting-dog category. Retrieve the verbatim body text of Laws XIX–XX from libro.uca.edu/vcode/vg8-4.htm or Gutenberg ebook 72551 before publishing.
3. **Make the Council of Agde (506) hunting-dog canon the ecclesiastical centerpiece**, quoting the Latin (*canes ad venandum aut accipitres*) and the graduated penalties, noting the canon-number variant (55 Sirmond / 52 MGH-Hispana), and contrasting it with the *absence* of any such canon at Toledo. This is the strongest single "hunting dog" datapoint in the batch.
4. **For the hunting-culture article, lead with the honest gap**: assemble the inferential case (Agde canon, villa continuity, eagle-iconography metalwork) but state clearly that no Visigothic hunting treatise and no securely published Visigothic hunting-dog faunal corpus were found. If deeper coverage is wanted, commission a Spanish-language search (Dialnet, CSIC's *Archivo Español de Arqueología*, and the zooarchaeology of sites such as Recópolis) for Visigothic *Canis* remains.
5. **Governing editorial rule for the whole batch**: do not assert lineage. The benchmark that would change this — and justify a stronger continuity claim — would be discovery of (a) a Visigothic-period text naming a sighthound/coursing dog, or (b) a peer-reviewed zooarchaeological study identifying gracile, cursorial (sighthound-morphology) dog remains in a securely dated Visigothic Iberian context. Absent either, the period remains a bridge of silence.

## Caveats
- **Verbatim body text of the Visigothic Code dog laws (VIII.IV.XIX–XX)** was confirmed at the rubric level from Scott's translation but the full paragraph text was not extracted in this research pass; retrieve it directly from libro.uca.edu or Gutenberg before quoting in the final article.
- **The completeness claim for the Councils of Toledo** (no hunting canon) rests on comprehensive scholarly summaries, not a canon-by-canon reading of all eighteen councils; it is very likely correct but should be verified against J. Vives, *Concilios visigóticos e hispano-romanos* (CSIC, 1963) for the final article.
- **Agde canon numbering** differs by edition (55 in the Sirmond tradition; 52/"LII" in the MGH edition of the Hispana rubric-list); the *text* (*canes ad venandum aut accipitres*) is stable.
- **Isidore is a compiler**, not an eyewitness to Iberian hunting; his dog lore descends from Pliny and Solinus. Nothing in it can be treated as direct evidence for 7th-century Spanish coursing.
- **The absence of hunting-dog evidence is an argument from silence** and should be presented as such: it establishes that the sources do not document coursing dogs in this period, not that coursing dogs did not exist.
