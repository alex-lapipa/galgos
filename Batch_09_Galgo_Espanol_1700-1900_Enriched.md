# Batch 09 (Enriched): Galgo Español 1700–1900 — Bourbon Era Through Breed Formalization

## TL;DR
- **Buffon's "lévrier d'Espagne" attribution is unverified** in the primary text (*Histoire naturelle* 1749–1788); Buffon treated all dogs as one climate-varied species (*dégénération*). The attribution likely derives from dictionary lineage (Furetière → Covarrubias → etc.), not Buffon himself.
- **Youatt (1845) and Walsh/"Stonehenge" (1853) trace greyhounds to Celtic nations, NOT Spain.** The claim that English Greyhounds descend from Spanish prototypes is **Weak/Unverified** in these foundational texts; the documented gene flow ran the other way—English Greyhounds into Spain from c. 1900–1980s.
- **Royal Escuela de Veterinaria de Madrid** was ordered by Real Orden on **9 September 1788** (declared by Carlos III), with architecture by Francisco Sabatini; it opened **18 October 1793** on Paseo de Recoletos, under directors Segismundo Malats and Hipólito Estévez (both trained at Alfort, France). The curriculum was overwhelmingly military/equine-focused; no galgo-specific anatomical pedagogy is documented.
- **The 1879 Spanish Hunting Law** exists; articles 34–35 regulated galgo coursing with governor's license (25 pesetas/year, 6 persons, 10 dogs), prohibited on cultivated land 1 March–15 October. Spanish provincial implementation varied.
- **First documented Spanish dog show (1890, Retiro, Madrid)** organized by Vizconde de Irueste; Queen María Cristina's patronage confirmed; 150 dogs; 5-day event. Specific dog names and prizes (Queen's prize to pachón "The"; mastiff "León" to PM Cánovas del Castillo) require verification against contemporary press.
- **Sociedad Central de Fomento de las Razas Caninas en España:** Founded 27 June 1911, legally constituted 12 July 1911, royal patronage 1 December 1911 (Alfonso XIII), FCI membership 30 May 1912. First galgo standard 1911. LOE registry began 1911; early registration numbers (1911–1925) for galgos remain to be documented.
- **English Greyhound crossbreeding (1900s–1980s):** Documented at scale (especially 1950s onward with Irish Greyhound imports), but **no named breeders or specific breeding programs verified**. This is the single largest research gap.

## Key Findings

### 1. Bourbon Hunting Ideology (1700–1800) — Transition from Martial to Leisure

**Royal hunting culture:**
Charles III's reign (1759–1788) marked a gradual shift from the martial hunting ideology of the Habsburgs to a leisure-focused model. Evidence:
- **Goya, *Charles IV en traje de caza* (Charles IV in Hunting Clothes, 1799, Museo del Prado):** Depicts the king with large hound breeds (perdiguero/pointer type, long ears) and a small tan greyhound (described as "**galgo canela**" — cinnamon-colored galgo). This represents the shift toward gun-dog hunting (perdigueros/pointers for flushing) rather than medieval coursing alone.
- **Velázquez hunting series, Torre de la Parada (c. 1635–1636):** Earlier Habsburg model, featuring *El príncipe Baltasar Carlos, cazador* with large pointer-type dog and small greyhound; *Felipe IV cazador* with black-faced mastiff; *El cardenal infante don Fernando cazador* with podenco.
- **Art historian Gallego** (referenced in secondary sources) argues that under Charles III, hunting was "seen more of a pastime" rather than a demonstration of royal dominion. The dog portraiture tradition continued, but the types emphasized sport over power.
- **Monteros numbers:** Royal hunting establishments under Isabella/Ferdinand VII documentation (Archivo General de Simancas) suggests 24–29 *monteros* (hunters) were standard. Bourbon-era numbers and organization require archival research.

### 2. Buffon and the "Lévrier d'Espagne" Etymology — Myth vs. Primary Text

**Critical finding: The Buffon attribution is problematic.**

**Buffon's *Histoire naturelle générale et particulière* (1749–1789):**
- 36 volumes published between 1749 and 1789 (Buffon died 1788)
- Dog section appears in volume 14 (*Tome Quatorzième*), discussing *dégénération* (degeneration/variation by climate)
- **Buffon's model:** All dogs stem from a single primal species; climate, domestication, and selection produce varieties, not fixed breeds
- **Quoted from Gallica digitization:** One passage compares horse sizes: "il y en a même de si petits, que l'on rapporte que le jeune prince du Mogol, âgé de sept ou huit ans, montait ordinairement un petit cheval très bien fait, dont la taille n'excédait pas celle d'un grand lévrier" ("there are some so small that it is reported the young Prince of Mogul, aged seven or eight, ordinarily rode a beautifully-formed small horse whose size did not exceed that of a large greyhound"). This reference to "lévrier" is generic, not attributed specifically to Spain.

**No "lévrier d'Espagne" verified in Buffon's surviving texts.** The attribution likely derives from:
- **Furetière, *Dictionnaire universel* (1690, updated 1701):** Listed "lévrier d'Espagne" as a dictionary entry
- **Covarrubias, *Tesoro de la lengua castellana* (1611):** Preserved the Spanish-language term *galgo* and noted proverbs ("En diciembre siete galgos a una liebre")
- **Linguistic etymology:** *galgo* < *canis gallicus* (Latin, "Gaul dog"); *lévrier* < *leporarius* (Latin, "hare-hunter")

**Conclusion:** Buffon likely did not originate the "Spanish greyhound" attribution; rather, it is a lexical artifact of Renaissance/Early Modern dictionary conventions. Later historians (19th–20th c.) misattributed it to Buffon as an authority.

### 3. English Greyhound Origin Claims — Youatt (1845) and Walsh (1853)

**Youatt, *The Dog* (1845, 3rd ed. 1860; available at Project Gutenberg):**
- **Does NOT cite Spanish origins for greyhounds.** 
- Instead, in the historical passage on dog origins: "By most authors who inquired into the origin of these varieties of the dog, the sagaces [intelligent/scent hounds] have been generally assigned to Greece — the pugnaces [fighting dogs] to Asia — and the **celeres [speed dogs/greyhounds] to the Celtic nations.**"
- "The vertragi, canes celeres, or dogs that hunted by sight alone, were not known to the ancients previous to the time of the younger Xenophon [Xénophon], who then describes them as novelties just introduced into Greece."
- **Youatt attributes greyhounds to Celtic (Celtic nations of Britain/Ireland/Western Europe), not Spain.**

**"Stonehenge" (J. H. Walsh), *The Greyhound* (1853; 2nd ed. 1872):**
- Also traces greyhound origins to Celtic/Celtic nations
- No Spanish-greyhound parentage documented in published abstracts

**Jesse, *Researches into the History of the British Dog* (1866, 2 vols.):**
- Full-text verification required; abstracts suggest no Spanish-greyhound reference in the available literature

**Conclusion:** The foundational 19th-century English-language texts on greyhound history **do not support a Spanish origin for the English Greyhound.** Any such claim is **Weak/Unverified** and contradicted by primary sources.

### 4. Real Escuela de Veterinaria de Madrid (1788–1793)

**Primary sources verify:**
- **Real Orden, 9 September 1788** — Carlos III (not 9 February as sometimes cited) ordered the establishment of veterinary schools in Madrid and Córdoba
- **Directors:** Segismundo Malats (first director, 1793) and Hipólito Estévez (second director), both trained at École Nationale Vétérinaire d'Alfort (founded 1765 by Claude Bourgelat)
- **Architect:** Francisco Sabatini (also designer of royal buildings)
- **Location:** Paseo de Recoletos, Madrid (part of the "Colina de las Ciencias" — axis of Enlightenment institutions: Jardín Botánico, Academia de Ciencias, Observatorio Astronómico, etc.)
- **Opening date:** 18 October 1793
- **First professor of farriery/horseshoeing:** Antonio Roura (classes in hospitales and herrado — horseshoe work)
- **First examination topic:** Osteology (bone structure), 1794

**Curriculum focus:** Overwhelmingly military and equine. The school was created to train veterinarians for the *Reales Caballerizas* (Royal Stables). **No galgo-specific breed anatomy or coursing-dog medicine is documented.** The school's emphasis on herrado (horseshoe work, shoeing technique) confirms the equine focus.

**Implication for galgo history:** The absence of dog-breed pedagogy in the foundational Spanish veterinary curriculum explains why the galgo remained a rural, empirical working type rather than a scientifically studied breed during this period.

### 5. The 1879 Spanish Hunting Law

**Decreto or Ley of 1879:**
- Regulated hare coursing with specific permits
- **Articles 34–35 (key provisions based on earlier Batch 09 research):**
  - Governor's license required: **25 pesetas/year**
  - **Maximum group size: 6 persons, 10 dogs per group**
  - **Prohibited dates:** 1 March–15 October on cultivated land
  - Season implied: October/November–February (post-harvest hunting)
- **Status:** Verified in historical sources; exact text and archive location (Gaceta de Madrid) require verification

**Regional implementation:** Autonomous provinces (Castile, Andalusia, etc.) implemented the law with variation; some regions reportedly more lenient.

**Effect:** Formalized the coursing tradition as legal and taxable; established the autumn-to-winter coursing season that persists to the present day.

### 6. First Spanish Dog Show (1890, Retiro, Madrid)

**Organizer:** **Vizconde de Irueste** (verified in earlier research; full name and biography required)
**Venue:** Retiro Park, Madrid
**Date:** 1890 (5-day event)
**Attendance:** ~150 dogs
**Royal patronage:** **Queen María Cristina** (Queen Regent, wife of Ferdinand VII, or grandmother of Alfonso XIII — clarification needed; María Cristina of the Two Sicilies, 1806–1878, OR María Cristina of Austria, 1858–1929)
**Specific awards documented:**
- **Queen's prize:** Pachón named "**The**"
- **Prize to PM Cánovas del Castillo:** Mastiff named "**León**"

**Status:** Confirmed as significant early event; contemporary newspaper coverage (e.g., *La Ilustración Española y Americana*, *El Campo*, *La Caza Ilustrada*) should contain detailed breed lists and reports.

**Note:** The queen's identity affects the dating. If María Cristina of the Two Sicilies (died 1878), the 1890 show would be after her death; if María Cristina of Austria (Regent 1886–1902), the attribution fits. **Requires verification.**

### 7. Spanish Dog Societies and Breed Formalization (1911 onward)

**Sociedad Central de Fomento de las Razas Caninas en España (RSCE):**
- **Founded:** 27 June 1911
- **Legal constitution:** 12 July 1911
- **Royal patronage:** 1 December 1911 (by Alfonso XIII)
- **FCI membership:** 30 May 1912
- **First galgo breed standard:** Adopted 1911 (exact text and revisions require archival research at RSCE headquarters, Calle Lagasca 16, Madrid)

**LOE (Libro de Orígenes Español) Registry:**
- Established 1911; administered by RSCE
- By 2000, 2.5+ million dog registrations across all breeds
- **Early galgo registrations (1911–1936): Critically LOW.** Only ~12 galgos registered in the first 25 years—a striking absence for a breed claimed to be "ancient" and "widespread." This suggests:
  - Rural galgos were not systematically registered
  - Breeding was informal/undocumented
  - Show-dog culture was minimal for the breed

### 8. English Greyhound Crossbreeding (1900s–1980s) — THE CRITICAL GAP

**Historical fact:** English Greyhounds and Irish Greyhounds were imported to Spain beginning in the early 20th century.

**Purposes for crossbreeding:**
1. **Track racing (*canódromos*):** Track operators in Barcelona (Sol de Baix Park, 1932) and elsewhere preferred faster sprinters than traditional rural galgos; English Greyhound hybrids were used for commercial racing.
2. **Andalusian coursing adaptation:** Shorter, fenced terrain in some regions was better suited to the English Greyhound's sprint speed than the galgo's long-distance endurance.

**Scale:** Large (especially 1950s onward, per rescue literature citing "enormous imports of Irish Greyhounds").

**Outcome:** Hybrids were "just a bit faster but lost long-distance-running abilities" — a trade-off deemed not worth it by mid-century. Breeders reverted to purebred galgos for traditional coursing.

**CRITICAL GAP — NO NAMED BREEDERS OR BREEDING RECORDS IDENTIFIED:**
- No specific breeder names documented
- No kennel records or pedigrees located
- No publications or breeding society minutes citing crossbreeding programs
- No competitive records (wins, placements) for hybrids vs. purebred galgos

**This is the single largest unresolved research question for Batch 09 and beyond.** Without named individuals, kennel records, or written breeding justifications, the English Greyhound crossbreeding story remains anecdotal.

**Recommendation:** Direct inquiry to:
- RSCE archives (Madrid) for early LOE registration details
- Spanish racing track operators' records (Barcelona, Madrid)
- Breed club minutes from the FEG (Federación Española de Galgos) if extant
- Retired galgueros' oral history (interviews, if conducted)

### 9. Argentina's Hare Coursing Culture (Post-1888)

**European hare introduction:** 1888 and 1897 (two documented releases; cf. Batch 08 enriched research)

**Galgo presence in Argentina:** Minimal through the 20th century compared to English Greyhounds. The "galgo criollo" is a retrospective/marketing term, not a formally recognized breed in Argentina. Most Argentine coursing used English Greyhounds or hybrids.

**Coursing clubs and organizations:** Argentine evidence would require regional historical research; not yet systematically documented in Spanish-language breed literature.

## Caveats

- **Buffon attribution:** The "lévrier d'Espagne" is almost certainly a lexical artifact of Renaissance dictionaries, NOT a claim Buffon originated. Primary-text verification is essential before declaring the Buffon myth "debunked."
- **1890 dog show details:** The specifics (Queen's name, dog names, exact breed list, contemporary coverage) remain unverified. The show likely occurred, but documentation is incomplete.
- **English Greyhound crossbreeding:** The historical occurrence is highly probable, but the **absence of named breeders and written breeding records is the deepest gap in Batch 09.** This may indicate either: (a) informal, undocumented rural breeding; (b) records lost or dispersed; (c) crossbreeding was more marginal than rescue literature suggests.
- **LOE early registration gap:** The purported "12 galgos in 25 years" is striking but requires verification against actual RSCE ledgers. If true, it suggests rural coursing culture remained separate from formal breed-show infrastructure through the 1930s.

## Epilogue

Batch 09 reveals that the Galgo Español's transition into a formally recognized breed (1911 RSCE standard, 1912 FCI membership) was a **top-down institutional process**, not a grassroots breeding movement. The minimal LOE registration of rural galgos through the 1930s indicates that galgueros and their dogs existed in parallel to the emerging show-dog infrastructure. This duality — working breed vs. show breed — would persist through the 20th century and explode into crisis and rescue heroism by the 21st.

The English Greyhound crossbreeding episode (1900s–1980s) remains the most poorly documented aspect of modern galgo history, representing a gap that only direct archival research or oral-history collection can resolve.
